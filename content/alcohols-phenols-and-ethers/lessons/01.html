Three bottles on a shelf

Picture a hospital shelf in three different centuries.

In 1846, a small glass globe of diethyl ether sits beside an operating table in Boston. A dentist named William Morton lets the patient breathe its vapour, and for the first time in history a surgeon cuts out a tumour while the patient sleeps through it.

In 1867, Joseph Lister sprays carbolic acid — what we now call phenol — over wounds and instruments, and deaths after surgery in his wards plunge.

Today, the bottle by the door is hand sanitiser: 70% ethanol or propan-2-ol.

An anaesthetic, an antiseptic and a sanitiser. Three very different jobs — and the same atom is doing the work in all of them: one oxygen with two single bonds.

Four questions, in order. What are they and what are they called? (Lesson 1.) How do we make them? (Lessons 2–3.) How do they behave — boiling, dissolving, giving up H⁺? (Lessons 4–5.) What reactions do they do? (Lessons 6–9.) Lesson 10 ties it into one map and a game. Every lesson is about half an hour, with a short breather in the middle — take it.

One oxygen, three personalities

Start from water, H–O–H, and swap out hydrogens:

  • Replace one H by an alkyl group: an alcohol, R–OH. Ethanol is $\ce{CH3CH2-OH}$.
  • Replace one H by an aryl group (the –OH sits directly on a benzene ring): a phenol, Ar–OH.
  • Replace both H atoms: an ether, R–O–R′. Diethyl ether is $\ce{CH3CH2-O-CH2CH3}$.

The –OH group of an alcohol or phenol is called the hydroxyl group. The carbon carrying it in an alcohol is the carbinol carbon — remember that word, because almost every rule in this chapter is about what is attached to it.

It is the same oxygen, but its neighbours change its mood. In an alcohol the O–H is ordinary. In a phenol the oxygen’s lone pair leaks into the benzene ring, which makes the O–H easier to break and the ring hyperactive. In an ether there is no O–H at all — which is exactly why ethers are so calm, and so useful as solvents.

Sorting alcohols

By the number of –OH groups. One, two or three: monohydric (ethanol), dihydric — ethane-1,2-diol, the antifreeze ethylene glycol — and trihydric, propane-1,2,3-triol or glycerol, the sweet, syrupy liquid in toothpaste and soap.

By the carbinol carbon. When the –OH sits on an sp³ carbon, count how many carbons are attached to that carbinol carbon:

Allylic and benzylic alcohols are also 1°, 2° or 3° — “allylic” and “benzylic” describe the neighbourhood, not a fourth class. They matter because a carbocation on that carbon is stabilised by resonance, so they react like tertiary alcohols in Lesson 6.

When the –OH sits on an sp² carbon you get a vinylic alcohol (an enol, like $\ce{CH2=CH-OH}$) — which quietly rearranges into an aldehyde or ketone and almost never survives in a bottle — or, if that sp² carbon is in a benzene ring, a phenol, which is perfectly stable.

2,2-Dimethylpropan-1-ol, $\ce{(CH3)3C-CH2OH}$, looks crowded, but the carbinol carbon ($\ce{CH2}$) carries only one carbon. It is a primary alcohol. Bulk next door does not change the class.

Sorting phenols and ethers

Phenols are sorted the same way by the number of –OH groups on the ring. The dihydric ones have old names that JEE still uses:

Catechol, Resorcinol, Quinol — in the order ortho, meta, para (1,2 → 1,3 → 1,4). Quinol is also called hydroquinone, the developer in old photographic film.

Ethers are simple (symmetrical) when both groups are the same, like $\ce{C2H5-O-C2H5}$, and mixed (unsymmetrical) when they differ, like $\ce{CH3-O-C2H5}$ or $\ce{C6H5-O-CH3}$.

Three small rules catch people out:

  1. With two or more –OH groups, the e of the alkane stays: ethane-1,2-diol, not “ethan-1,2-diol”.
  2. In a ring, the carbinol carbon is C-1 and the locant “1” is usually dropped: cyclohexanol, 2-methylcyclopentanol.
  3. –OH outranks C=C and C≡C, so it gets the suffix and the lowest number. If the double and triple bonds then tie for locants, the double bond gets the lower one. (–OH is in turn outranked by –COOH, –CHO and C=O; with those present it becomes a hydroxy prefix.)

Ethers are alkoxyalkanes: the larger group is the parent and the smaller becomes an alkoxy prefix. $\ce{CH3-O-CH2CH3}$ is methoxyethane (common name ethyl methyl ether — common names list the groups alphabetically); $\ce{C6H5OCH3}$ is methoxybenzene, better known as anisole; $\ce{C6H5OC2H5}$ is ethoxybenzene, or phenetole; $\ce{CH3OCH2CH2OCH3}$ is 1,2-dimethoxyethane.

What the molecules actually look like

In all three families the oxygen is sp³ hybridised: two bonds and two lone pairs, roughly tetrahedral. But none of the angles is the ideal 109.5°, and the reasons are worth knowing.

  • Alcohols (methanol): C–O–H is 108.9°, slightly less than tetrahedral. The two lone pairs repel the bonding pairs more strongly than bonding pairs repel each other, and squeeze the angle.
  • Phenols: C–O–H is about 109°, but the striking number is the C–O bond length: 136 pm, against 142 pm in methanol. Two reasons: the oxygen’s lone pair is partly shared with the ring, giving the C–O bond a little double-bond character, and the carbon is sp², with more s-character, so it holds the oxygen closer.
  • Ethers (methoxymethane): C–O–C is 111.7°, more than tetrahedral, because the two bulky alkyl groups push each other apart. The C–O bond (141 pm) is about the same as in alcohols.

C–O–H in methanol 108.9° · C–O–C in methoxymethane 111.7° · C–O in phenol 136 pm vs 142 pm in methanol. The shorter, stronger phenol C–O bond is why phenols, unlike alcohols, almost never lose their –OH in a substitution reaction.
