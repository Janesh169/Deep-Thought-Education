Three roads to an –OH

Every laboratory route to an alcohol in your syllabus is one of three moves. Learn them as moves, not as a list, and the whole lesson collapses into one picture:

Faced with “make this alcohol”, look at the carbinol carbon. Did it get its –OH from a C=C? Its hydrogen from a C=O? One of its carbon groups from a Grignard reagent? Working backwards from the –OH is how every JEE conversion question in this chapter is solved.

Road 1a: adding water the Markovnikov way

Alkenes do not react with plain water, but with a little acid as catalyst they add H–OH across the double bond:

$$\ce{CH3-CH=CH2 + H2O ->[H+] CH3-CH(OH)-CH3}$$

The –OH ends up on the more substituted carbon — Markovnikov’s rule — and the mechanism tells you why.

Step 1 — protonation (slow). $\ce{H3O+}$ attacks the π bond as an electrophile, putting H on one carbon and leaving a carbocation on the other. It chooses the more stable carbocation: for propene, the 2° one.

$$\ce{CH3-CH=CH2 + H3O+ -> CH3-\overset{+}{C}H-CH3 + H2O}$$

Step 2 — water attacks. A water molecule uses a lone pair to bond to the carbocation, giving a protonated alcohol.

$$\ce{CH3-\overset{+}{C}H-CH3 + H2O -> CH3-CH(\overset{+}{O}H2)-CH3}$$

Step 3 — deprotonation. Another water molecule takes the extra proton, giving back $\ce{H3O+}$, so the acid is only a catalyst.

$$\ce{CH3-CH(\overset{+}{O}H2)-CH3 + H2O -> CH3-CH(OH)-CH3 + H3O+}$$

Because step 1 makes a free carbocation, a 1,2-shift of H or $\ce{CH3}$ can turn a 2° cation into a more stable 3° one. Hydrating 3,3-dimethylbut-1-ene gives mainly 2,3-dimethylbutan-2-ol, not the “obvious” 3,3-dimethylbutan-2-ol. Whenever a C=C sits next to a carbon carrying extra methyl groups, check for a shift.

 3,3-Dimethylbut-1-ene, $\ce{(CH3)3C-CH=CH2}$, is warmed with dilute $\ce{H2SO4}$. The major product is: 3,3-Dimethylbutan-1-ol 3,3-Dimethylbutan-2-ol 2,3-Dimethylbutan-2-ol 2,2-Dimethylbutan-1-ol Protonate to the 2° carbocation first. Is there a methyl group next door that could move and leave a 3° cation behind?

Protonation gives the 2° cation $\ce{(CH3)3C-\overset{+}{C}H-CH3}$. A methyl group on the neighbouring carbon shifts across with its bonding pair (a 1,2-methyl shift), giving the 3° cation $\ce{(CH3)2\overset{+}{C}-CH(CH3)-CH3}$. Water attacks that: $\ce{(CH3)2C(OH)-CH(CH3)2}$, which is 2,3-dimethylbutan-2-ol. Answer (C).

Road 1b: adding water the other way round

Hydroboration–oxidation adds the same H and OH across the double bond, but with the opposite orientation:

$$\ce{CH3-CH=CH2 ->[(i) B2H6][(ii) H2O2,\ OH^-] CH3-CH2-CH2OH}$$

Diborane, $\ce{(BH3)2}$, adds H–B across the C=C. Boron is the electron-poor end, so it goes to the less substituted, less crowded carbon, and hydrogen goes to the other. Three such additions give a trialkylborane $\ce{(CH3CH2CH2)3B}$. Alkaline hydrogen peroxide then replaces each C–B bond by C–OH.

The result looks like water added against Markovnikov’s rule. There is no carbocation at any stage, so there is no rearrangement, and the yield is excellent.

$\ce{Hg(OAc)2}$ in water/THF, then $\ce{NaBH4}$, adds water the Markovnikov way like acid does, but through a bridged mercurinium ion instead of a free carbocation — so, again, no rearrangement.

Road 2: reduce a C=O

Add two hydrogen atoms across a carbonyl group and the oxygen becomes –OH:

  • Aldehydes → primary alcohols; ketones → secondary alcohols. Reagents: $\ce{H2}$ over Pt, Pd or Ni (catalytic hydrogenation), or the hydride donors sodium borohydride $\ce{NaBH4}$ and lithium aluminium hydride $\ce{LiAlH4}$.
  • Carboxylic acids → primary alcohols, but only with the powerful $\ce{LiAlH4}$. It is expensive, so industry first turns the acid into an ester and then hydrogenates the ester over a catalyst.
$$\ce{RCHO ->[H2,\ Pd] RCH2OH} \qquad \ce{R-CO-R' ->[NaBH4] R-CH(OH)-R'} \qquad \ce{RCOOH ->[(i) LiAlH4][(ii) H2O] RCH2OH}$$

$\ce{H2/Ni}$ reduces C=C and C=O. $\ce{NaBH4}$ is gentle: aldehydes and ketones only — not C=C, not acids or esters. $\ce{LiAlH4}$ reduces aldehydes, ketones, acids and esters, but leaves an isolated C=C alone. Picking the right one lets you reduce one group and spare another.

 Which reagent converts propenal, $\ce{CH2=CH-CHO}$, into prop-2-en-1-ol, $\ce{CH2=CH-CH2OH}$? $\ce{H2}$, Ni $\ce{NaBH4}$ $\ce{H2O/H+}$ (i) $\ce{B2H6}$, (ii) $\ce{H2O2/OH-}$

We must reduce the C=O and leave the C=C. Hydrogen over nickel would reduce both, giving propan-1-ol. $\ce{NaBH4}$ delivers hydride only to the carbonyl carbon. (C) and (D) attack the C=C instead. Answer (B).

Roll your shoulders back twice and unclench your jaw — most of us study with both slightly locked.

Next up is the most powerful tool in this lesson: a reagent that builds new carbon–carbon bonds. Worth arriving fresh for.

Road 3: let a Grignard reagent build it

A Grignard reagent, R–MgX, has a carbon–magnesium bond so polar that the carbon behaves like a carbanion, $\ce{R^-}$. It attacks the carbon of a C=O (nucleophilic addition), and water then turns the magnesium alkoxide into an alcohol:

$$\ce{>C=O + R-MgX -> >C(R)-OMgX ->[H2O] >C(R)-OH + Mg(OH)X}$$

Because R joins the carbinol carbon, the carbonyl compound you pick decides the class of alcohol:

Carbonyl compoundAlcohol formedExample
Methanal, HCHOprimary$\ce{HCHO + CH3MgBr ->} \ce{CH3CH2OH}$
Any other aldehyde, RCHOsecondary$\ce{CH3CHO + CH3MgBr ->} \ce{(CH3)2CHOH}$
Ketone, R₂COtertiary$\ce{(CH3)2CO + CH3MgBr ->} \ce{(CH3)3COH}$
Ester, RCOOR′ (two RMgX)tertiary, with two identical new groups$\ce{CH3COOC2H5 + 2CH3MgBr ->} \ce{(CH3)3COH}$
Ethylene oxide (epoxide)primary, chain longer by two carbons$\ce{CH3MgBr ->} \ce{CH3CH2CH2OH}$

(Every row ends with hydrolysis by water or dilute acid.) An ester reacts twice: the first $\ce{RMgX}$ adds and kicks out the $\ce{-OR'}$ group to give a ketone, which immediately takes a second $\ce{RMgX}$.

R–MgX is also a very strong base. Any compound with an acidic H — water, alcohols, phenols, carboxylic acids, $\ce{NH3}$, amines, even a terminal alkyne — simply gives up that proton and turns the Grignard reagent into an alkane: $\ce{CH3MgBr + H2O -> CH4 + Mg(OH)Br}$. That is why Grignard reactions are run in perfectly dry ether.

Two alcohols the world makes by the tanker

Methanol was once called wood spirit, because it came from heating wood without air. Today it is made from carbon monoxide and hydrogen:

$$\ce{CO + 2H2 ->[ZnO-Cr2O3][200-300\ atm,\ 573-673\ K] CH3OH}$$

It is a solvent and fuel — and it is poisonous: a small amount can cause blindness, a larger one death (Lesson 7 explains why).

Ethanol is made by fermentation of sugars from molasses, sugarcane or fruit such as grapes. Two enzymes from yeast do it in turn:

$$\ce{C12H22O11 + H2O ->[invertase] \underset{glucose}{C6H12O6} + \underset{fructose}{C6H12O6}}$$ $$\ce{C6H12O6 ->[zymase] 2C2H5OH + 2CO2}$$

Fermentation runs without air. Let oxygen in and other microbes oxidise the ethanol to ethanoic acid — which is how wine turns into vinegar. Ethanol is also made industrially by hydrating ethene.

Ethanol sold for industry is made undrinkable by adding a little copper sulphate (for colour) and pyridine (for a foul smell). This is called denaturation of alcohol.

 In the fermentation of cane sugar to ethanol, the enzymes act in which order? Zymase, then invertase Invertase, then zymase Diastase, then maltase Zymase alone does both steps

Sucrose is first split into glucose and fructose by invertase; zymase then ferments the hexoses to ethanol and carbon dioxide. (Diastase and maltase belong to the fermentation of starch, which must first be broken down to sugars.) Answer (B).
  • Hydration of alkenes: $\ce{H2O/H+}$ → Markovnikov, through a carbocation (watch for rearrangement); $\ce{B2H6}$ then $\ce{H2O2/OH-}$ → anti-Markovnikov orientation, no rearrangement; oxymercuration → Markovnikov, no rearrangement.
  • Reduction: aldehyde → 1°, ketone → 2°, acid or ester → 1°. $\ce{NaBH4}$ for aldehydes and ketones only; $\ce{LiAlH4}$ for acids and esters too.
  • Grignard: HCHO → 1°, RCHO → 2°, ketone → 3°, ester (2 RMgX) → 3°, epoxide → 1° (+2 C). Any acidic H kills the reagent.
  • Methanol from $\ce{CO + 2H2}$; ethanol by fermentation (invertase, then zymase, no air); denatured with $\ce{CuSO4}$ and pyridine.
