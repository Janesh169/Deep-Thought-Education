Three molecules, the same weight, wildly different

Here are three molecules of almost exactly the same mass. On a scale you could not tell them apart. In a flask you could not confuse them:

Propane, $\ce{CH3CH2CH3}$44231 Ka gas (camping-stove fuel)
Methoxymethane, $\ce{CH3OCH3}$46248 Ka gas
Ethanol, $\ce{CH3CH2OH}$46351 Ka liquid you can pour

The ether boils a little above the alkane. The alcohol boils more than 100 K above both. Same mass, same size — so something besides ordinary van der Waals attraction is holding ethanol molecules together.

The handshake only alcohols can make

An O–H bond is strongly polar: the hydrogen carries a sizeable δ+, and the oxygen carries δ− and two lone pairs. The H of one ethanol molecule is pulled towards a lone pair on the O of the next — a hydrogen bond. Each alcohol molecule is both a donor (through its O–H) and an acceptor (through its lone pairs), so the liquid becomes a loose, constantly re-forming network. To boil it, you must tear that network apart, which costs extra energy.

  • Alkanes: no polar bonds worth mentioning, no hydrogen bonds — the lowest boiling points.
  • Ethers: a polar, bent C–O–C, so slightly higher than alkanes; but there is no O–H, so ether molecules cannot hydrogen-bond to each other. Their boiling points stay close to those of alkanes.
  • Alcohols and phenols: hydrogen bonds — much higher boiling points. Phenol (M = 94) boils at 455 K; toluene (M = 92) at 384 K.

Two trends within alcohols. Boiling point rises with the number of carbons (bigger molecules, more van der Waals attraction) and falls with branching (a rounder molecule has less surface in contact with its neighbours). The four butanols show it beautifully: butan-1-ol 391 K, 2-methylpropan-1-ol 381 K, butan-2-ol 373 K, 2-methylpropan-2-ol 355 K.

All four hydrogen-bond, so the difference comes from shape. The most branched isomer, 2-methylpropan-2-ol, is nearly spherical: least surface contact, weakest van der Waals attraction, and its crowded –OH also hydrogen-bonds less well. It boils at 355 K, against 391 K for butan-1-ol. Answer (D).

Why ethanol mixes with water and butanol barely does

To dissolve in water, a molecule has to break into water’s own hydrogen-bond network — and it pays its way by forming new hydrogen bonds with water. The –OH of an alcohol does exactly that, so the lower alcohols mix with water in all proportions.

But the alkyl part is hydrophobic: it cannot hydrogen-bond, and water molecules have to arrange themselves awkwardly around it. As the carbon chain grows, that penalty wins. Methanol, ethanol and the propanols are fully miscible; butan-1-ol dissolves only about 9 g per 100 mL; hexan-1-ol barely at all.

Ethers cannot donate a hydrogen bond, but their oxygen can still accept one from water. So small ethers dissolve about as well as alcohols of the same mass: diethyl ether about 7.5 g and butan-1-ol about 9 g per 100 mL, while pentane is essentially immiscible.

Boiling point depends on hydrogen bonds between molecules of the substance — ethers have none, so they boil like alkanes. Solubility depends on hydrogen bonds with water — ethers can accept them, so they dissolve like alcohols. Same molecule, two different answers.

I is true. The ether oxygen accepts hydrogen bonds from water. With only two carbons the hydrophobic part is small, so dimethyl ether dissolves freely; with four carbons, diethyl ether dissolves only to about 7.5 g per 100 mL.

II is true. Sodium reacts with any O–H: $\ce{2C2H5OH + 2Na -> 2C2H5ONa + H2}$, so it would destroy ethanol. Diethyl ether has no acidic hydrogen, so sodium removes only the traces of water in it. Answer (D).

Then one last idea for this lesson — a hydrogen bond that points inward instead of outward.

The nitrophenol that runs away with steam

In o-nitrophenol, the –OH and the –NO₂ are neighbours. The O–H hydrogen bonds to an oxygen of its own nitro group, closing a six-membered ring. This is intramolecular hydrogen bonding, also called chelation. With its hydrogen “already taken”, the molecule hardly hydrogen-bonds to its neighbours or to water.

In p-nitrophenol the groups are too far apart for that, so the O–H bonds to other molecules — intermolecular hydrogen bonding — and to water.

  • Boiling points at similar mass: alcohol ≫ ether > alkane. Alcohols hydrogen-bond to each other; ethers cannot (no O–H).
  • Within alcohols, b.p. rises with chain length and falls with branching.
  • Solubility comes from hydrogen bonds with water. Alcohols donate and accept; ethers accept, so small ethers dissolve like alcohols of the same mass. Longer carbon chains make both less soluble.
  • o-Nitrophenol: intramolecular H-bond → lower b.p., less water-soluble, steam volatile. p-Nitrophenol: intermolecular → higher b.p.
