Drop in a piece of sodium

Put a small piece of sodium into ethanol and it fizzes, more gently than in water: hydrogen gas, and a solution of sodium ethoxide. Phenol does the same. Diethyl ether does nothing — which is why chemists store ether over sodium wire to keep it dry.

$$\ce{2R-OH + 2Na -> 2R-O^-Na+ + H2 ^}$$

So alcohols and phenols are acids, in the Brønsted sense: they can give up the H of their O–H. But try a stronger test. Shake each with aqueous sodium hydroxide:

  • Phenol dissolves, as sodium phenoxide: $$\ce{C6H5OH + NaOH -> C6H5O^-Na+ + H2O}$$
  • Ethanol does not react appreciably — the equilibrium lies almost entirely on the alcohol side.

Phenol is about a million times more acidic than ethanol. Explaining that factor is the whole of this lesson.

An acid HA is strong when its conjugate base $\ce{A-}$ is stable. Anything that spreads out or soothes the negative charge left on the oxygen makes the acid stronger; anything that piles more electron density onto it makes the acid weaker.

Alcohols: why bigger groups mean weaker acids

Alkyl groups push electron density towards the oxygen (+I effect). In the alkoxide ion $\ce{RO-}$ that oxygen already carries a full negative charge, so extra density destabilises it. More alkyl groups on the carbinol carbon, more destabilisation:

$$\text{acidity:}\quad 1^\circ > 2^\circ > 3^\circ \qquad \ce{CH3CH2OH} > \ce{(CH3)2CHOH} > \ce{(CH3)3COH}$$

(Crowding around the oxygen also stops water from solvating the alkoxide ion well, which pushes the same way.)

Alcohols versus water. Alkoxide ions are stronger bases than hydroxide: $\ce{RO- + H2O -> ROH + OH-}$ goes to the right. So water is the better proton donor and alcohols are, in general, weaker acids than water (ethanol pKa ≈ 15.9 vs water 15.7). Methanol is the borderline case, very close to water.

Alcohols are also weak bases: the oxygen lone pairs accept a proton from a strong acid, giving $\ce{R-OH2+}$. That protonation is the first step of almost every reaction in Lesson 6.

 Arrange in decreasing order of acidity: (I) 2-methylpropan-2-ol, (II) ethanol, (III) propan-2-ol. I > III > II II > III > I III > II > I II > I > III

Ethanol is primary, propan-2-ol secondary, 2-methylpropan-2-ol tertiary. Each extra alkyl group on the carbinol carbon pushes more electron density onto the alkoxide oxygen (and hinders its solvation), destabilising the conjugate base. Acidity: 1° > 2° > 3°, so II > III > I. Answer (B).

Phenol: the charge that has somewhere to go

Two things make phenol the stronger acid.

1. The –OH sits on an sp² carbon. An sp² carbon is more electronegative than an sp³ one, so it pulls electron density away from the oxygen and makes the O–H bond more polar.

2. The phenoxide ion is stabilised by resonance. In ethoxide the negative charge is stuck on one oxygen. In phenoxide it is spread over the oxygen and three ring carbons:

Phenol itself also has resonance structures, but they separate charge (+ on O, − on the ring), which costs energy. Phenoxide’s structures carry only the one negative charge, just moved around. So resonance stabilises the ion more than the acid, and the equilibrium shifts towards ionisation.

Think of the negative charge as heat in a metal rod: concentrated in one spot it is fierce; spread along the rod it is gentle. Phenoxide spreads its charge over four atoms. Ethoxide keeps it all on one oxygen — and pushes even more onto it from the ethyl group.

Substituents: tuning phenol’s acidity

Because the charge reaches the ortho and para carbons, groups at those positions can help or hurt it most.

  • Electron-withdrawing groups (–NO₂, –CN, –Cl, –CHO) stabilise phenoxide → stronger acid. At o/p, –NO₂ pulls the charge onto its own oxygens by resonance (−R) as well as by induction (−I). At meta only −I acts, so the boost is smaller.
  • Electron-donating groups (–CH₃, –OCH₃, –NH₂) destabilise phenoxide → weaker acid.

Then: the bicarbonate test — the quickest way, in a lab or an exam, to tell a phenol from a carboxylic acid.

The bicarbonate test

Carbonic acid has pKa ≈ 6.4. Any acid stronger than that (lower pKa) reacts with sodium bicarbonate and releases bubbles of $\ce{CO2}$; anything weaker does not.

  • Carboxylic acids (pKa ≈ 4–5): fizz.
  • Picric acid (0.4) and 2,4-dinitrophenol (4.0): fizz.
  • Phenol (10.0) and cresols: no fizz — but they dissolve in NaOH.
  • Alcohols: react with neither.
$$\text{acid strength:}\quad \ce{RCOOH} > \ce{H2CO3} > \text{phenol} > \ce{H2O} > \ce{R-OH}$$ Which of the following dissolve in aqueous $\ce{NaHCO3}$ with effervescence? Benzoic acid Phenol 2,4,6-Trinitrophenol p-Cresol

Only acids stronger than carbonic acid (pKa 6.4) release $\ce{CO2}$. Benzoic acid (4.2) and picric acid (0.4) do; phenol (10.0) and p-cresol (10.2) are far too weak. Answer: A, C.

All three phenols beat both alcohols. Among the phenols, the para-nitro group stabilises phenoxide (E strongest), and the para-methoxy group destabilises it by +R (D weaker than phenol C). Among the alcohols, the 1° methanol beats the 3° 2-methylpropan-2-ol. So E > C > D > A > B. Answer (A).

 Phenol has pKa 10.0 and ethanol has pKa 15.9. The ratio $K_a(\text{phenol})/K_a(\text{ethanol})$ is close to $10^n$. What is $n$, to the nearest integer? $K_a = 10^{-\mathrm{p}K_a}$.

$\dfrac{K_a(\text{phenol})}{K_a(\text{ethanol})} = \dfrac{10^{-10.0}}{10^{-15.9}} = 10^{5.9} \approx 10^{6}$. Phenol is roughly a million times the stronger acid. n = 6.

 A compound $\ce{C7H8O}$ dissolves in aqueous NaOH but not in aqueous $\ce{NaHCO3}$, and gives a violet colour with neutral $\ce{FeCl3}$. It is: Benzyl alcohol, $\ce{C6H5CH2OH}$ Anisole, $\ce{C6H5OCH3}$ p-Cresol, $\ce{CH3C6H4OH}$ None of these

All three named compounds are $\ce{C7H8O}$. Dissolving in NaOH but not in bicarbonate means an acid of phenol strength. The violet colour with $\ce{FeCl3}$ is the test for a phenolic –OH (Lesson 8). Benzyl alcohol is an alcohol (too weak for NaOH); anisole has no O–H at all. Answer (C).
  • Alcohols and phenols release $\ce{H2}$ with Na. Only phenols react with NaOH; only acids stronger than $\ce{H2CO3}$ (carboxylic acids, picric acid) react with $\ce{NaHCO3}$.
  • Alcohols: acidity 1° > 2° > 3° (+I destabilises the alkoxide); generally weaker acids than water.
  • Phenol (pKa 10) ≫ ethanol (15.9): sp² carbon, and the phenoxide charge is delocalised onto o and p carbons.
  • Electron-withdrawing groups at o/p raise acidity most (−R and −I); at meta only −I. Electron donors lower it — except that a meta –OCH₃ raises it.
