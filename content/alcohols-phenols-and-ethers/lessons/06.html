Two bonds you can break

Every reaction of an alcohol breaks one of two bonds:

  • O–H: the alcohol keeps its oxygen and acts as a nucleophile through it. Reaction with sodium (Lesson 5) and esterification are of this kind.
  • C–O: the alcohol loses its –OH and the carbon is attacked or a C=C forms. Halide formation and dehydration are of this kind.

$\ce{OH-}$ is a strong base and does not want to leave. Every C–O-breaking reaction first converts –OH into something that leaves happily: protonate it to $\ce{-OH2+}$, which leaves as neutral water, or tie it to phosphorus, sulphur or zinc. Spot the disguise and the mechanism writes itself.

Breaking O–H: esterification

Alcohols and phenols react with carboxylic acids, acid chlorides and acid anhydrides to form esters:

$$\ce{R-OH + R'-COOH <=>[H+] R'-COOR + H2O}$$ $$\ce{R-OH + R'-COCl ->[pyridine] R'-COOR + HCl}$$

With a carboxylic acid the reaction is reversible and needs a little concentrated $\ce{H2SO4}$; removing water as it forms drives it forward. With an acid chloride, a base such as pyridine mops up the HCl, pulling the reaction to completion.

The most famous example: acetylating the phenolic –OH of salicylic acid with ethanoic anhydride gives acetylsalicylic acid — aspirin, an analgesic, anti-inflammatory and antipyretic. Introducing a $\ce{CH3CO-}$ group like this is called acetylation; introducing $\ce{C6H5CO-}$ with benzoyl chloride in aqueous NaOH is benzoylation (the Schotten–Baumann reaction).

In the reaction

$$\ce{ROH + CH3COCl ->[pyridine] CH3COOR + HCl}$$

the main job of pyridine is to:

 catalyse the cleavage of the C–O bond of the alcohol neutralise the HCl formed, shifting the equilibrium towards the ester oxidise the alcohol so that it reacts faster remove water formed in the reaction

Pyridine is a base. It removes the HCl as pyridinium chloride, so the reverse reaction cannot happen and the ester forms completely. No water is formed with an acid chloride, and the alcohol’s C–O bond is never broken: the O–H is. Answer (B).

Breaking C–O: turning alcohols into halides

Alcohols react with hydrogen halides to give alkyl halides:

$$\ce{R-OH + H-X -> R-X + H2O}$$

Two orders of reactivity, both worth memorising:

  • Halogen acid: HI > HBr > HCl.
  • Alcohol: 3° > 2° > 1°. The acid protonates the –OH, water leaves, and the faster the carbocation forms, the faster the reaction. Tertiary alcohols react by $\mathrm{S_N1}$.

Hydrochloric acid alone is sluggish, so anhydrous $\ce{ZnCl2}$ is added. Zinc, a Lewis acid, grabs the oxygen and helps the C–O bond break. Concentrated HCl + anhydrous $\ce{ZnCl2}$ is the Lucas reagent, and it is the classic way to tell 1°, 2° and 3° alcohols apart.

Alcohols dissolve in the Lucas reagent, but alkyl chlorides do not. So the moment the chloride forms, the clear solution turns cloudy (turbid):

Other ways to make alkyl halides

Phosphorus halides and thionyl chloride also replace –OH by halogen:

$$\ce{3R-OH + PX3 -> 3R-X + H3PO3} \qquad \ce{R-OH + PCl5 -> R-Cl + POCl3 + HCl}$$ $$\ce{R-OH + SOCl2 -> R-Cl + SO2 ^ + HCl ^}$$

Thionyl chloride is the favourite: both by-products are gases that escape, leaving pure alkyl chloride.

The C–O bond of phenol is short and strong (partial double bond, sp² carbon — Lesson 1), and an aryl cation is far too unstable to form. So phenols do not react with HX, and you cannot make chlorobenzene from phenol with HCl or the Lucas reagent.

 Which alcohols give turbidity with the Lucas reagent immediately at room temperature? 2-Methylbutan-2-ol Prop-2-en-1-ol, $\ce{CH2=CH-CH2OH}$ Butan-1-ol Phenylmethanol, $\ce{C6H5CH2OH}$

(A) is tertiary: immediate. (B) and (D) are primary on paper, but their carbocations — allyl and benzyl — are stabilised by resonance with the neighbouring π system, so they react as fast as tertiary alcohols. (C) is an ordinary primary alcohol: no turbidity at room temperature. Answer: A, B, D.

Losing water: dehydration to alkenes

Heat an alcohol with a protic acid and it loses a molecule of water, forming a C=C. How hard you have to push depends on the class:

$$\ce{CH3CH2OH ->[conc.\ H2SO4][443\ K] CH2=CH2 + H2O}$$ $$\ce{CH3CH(OH)CH3 ->[85\%\ H3PO4][440\ K] CH3CH=CH2 + H2O}$$ $$\ce{(CH3)3COH ->[20\%\ H3PO4][358\ K] (CH3)2C=CH2 + H2O}$$

Concentrated acid and 443 K for a primary alcohol; a weaker acid at 440 K for secondary; dilute acid at only 358 K for tertiary. Ease of dehydration: 3° > 2° > 1° — the same carbocation order again.

Step 1 — protonation (fast). The alcohol’s oxygen takes a proton from the acid: $\ce{CH3CH2OH + H+ <=> CH3CH2\overset{+}{O}H2}$. This is the initiation step.

Step 2 — the carbocation forms (slow, rate-determining). Water leaves: $\ce{CH3CH2\overset{+}{O}H2 <=> CH3\overset{+}{C}H2 + H2O}$.

Step 3 — a proton is lost (fast). A base removes an H from a carbon next to the cationic centre, and its bonding pair becomes the π bond: $\ce{CH3\overset{+}{C}H2 -> CH2=CH2 + H+}$. The acid is regenerated: it is a catalyst.

(For a primary alcohol a free 1° carbocation is very unstable, and steps 2 and 3 really happen together. JEE and NCERT use the three-step picture above, and for 2° and 3° alcohols it is accurate.)

When more than one alkene can form, the more substituted, more stable alkene is the major product (Saytzeff’s rule): butan-2-ol gives mainly but-2-ene, not but-1-ene. And because step 2 makes a carbocation, a 1,2-shift of H or $\ce{CH3}$ can turn it into a more stable one first — exactly as in Lesson 2.

Which proton can the new 3° cation lose to give the most substituted alkene?

Losing water gives the 2° cation $\ce{(CH3)3C-\overset{+}{C}H-CH3}$. A 1,2-methyl shift makes the 3° cation $\ce{(CH3)2\overset{+}{C}-CH(CH3)2}$. Losing the H on the neighbouring CH gives the tetrasubstituted alkene $\ce{(CH3)2C=C(CH3)2}$ — the most stable, so the major product (2,3-dimethylbut-1-ene is the minor one). Answer (C).

 The main product of the reaction $\ce{C6H5CH2CH(OH)CH(CH3)2 ->[conc.\ H2SO4]}$ ? is: $\ce{C6H5CH2CH=C(CH3)2}$ $\ce{C6H5CH=CH-CH(CH3)2}$ $\ce{C6H5CH2CH2C(CH3)=CH2}$ $\ce{C6H5CH2CH(CH3)CH=CH2}$ Several alkenes can form. Which one is most stable? A C=C can be stabilised by alkyl groups — or by something better.

Hot concentrated acid lets the possible alkenes form and interconvert, so the most stable alkene wins. A C=C that is conjugated with the benzene ring is stabilised more than one that merely carries an extra alkyl group. So the main product is $\ce{C6H5CH=CH-CH(CH3)2}$ (mostly the trans isomer), rather than the trisubstituted but unconjugated (A). Answer (B).
  • O–H cleavage: esterification (acid ⇌ ester with $\ce{H+}$; acid chloride + pyridine goes to completion). Acetylating salicylic acid gives aspirin.
  • C–O cleavage with HX: HI > HBr > HCl; 3° > 2° > 1°. Lucas reagent (conc. HCl + anhydrous $\ce{ZnCl2}$): 3° at once, 2° in ~5 min, 1° not at room temperature. Benzylic and allylic act like 3°. $\ce{SOCl2}$ gives pure RCl. Phenols do not react.
  • Dehydration: 1° conc. $\ce{H2SO4}$ 443 K; 2° 85% $\ce{H3PO4}$ 440 K; 3° 20% $\ce{H3PO4}$ 358 K. Protonation → carbocation (slow) → loss of $\ce{H+}$. Saytzeff alkene; watch for 1,2-shifts and conjugation.
