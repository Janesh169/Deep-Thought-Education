Orange to green at the roadside

The first police breathalysers of the 1950s were glass tubes packed with crystals of potassium dichromate soaked in sulphuric acid — bright orange. A driver blew through the tube; any ethanol in the breath was oxidised, and the dichromate ($\ce{Cr2O7^2-}$, orange) was reduced to $\ce{Cr^3+}$, green. The further the green stain crept along the tube, the more the driver had drunk.

That colour change is the reaction of this lesson. It also hides a test: a tertiary alcohol would have left the tube orange.

Oxidation is taking hydrogen away

Oxidising an alcohol removes two hydrogens: the one on oxygen and one on the carbinol carbon, leaving a C=O. So what happens depends on how many hydrogens that carbon has:

  • Primary → aldehyde → carboxylic acid. Strong oxidants — acidified $\ce{KMnO4}$ or acidified $\ce{K2Cr2O7}$ — go all the way to the acid. To stop at the aldehyde, use $\ce{CrO3}$ in an anhydrous medium or, better, pyridinium chlorochromate (PCC), $\ce{C5H5NH+CrO3Cl-}$, in dichloromethane.
  • Secondary → ketone, with $\ce{CrO3}$, chromic acid or PCC. Ketones resist further mild oxidation.
  • Tertiary: no H on the carbinol carbon, so no oxidation under mild conditions. Forced with hot $\ce{KMnO4}$, the carbon skeleton itself breaks and you get a mixture of acids with fewer carbons.

In water, an aldehyde adds a molecule of water to form a hydrate, $\ce{RCH(OH)2}$ — which looks like an alcohol to the oxidant and is oxidised again, to the acid. Take water away (anhydrous $\ce{CrO3}$, PCC in $\ce{CH2Cl2}$) and the aldehyde has nothing to hide behind.

 The most suitable reagent for the conversion $\ce{R-CH2-OH -> R-CHO}$ is: $\ce{KMnO4}$ $\ce{K2Cr2O7}$ $\ce{CrO3}$ PCC (pyridinium chlorochromate)

Permanganate and dichromate (in aqueous acid) carry a primary alcohol straight on to the carboxylic acid. $\ce{CrO3}$ stops at the aldehyde only if the medium is strictly anhydrous. PCC is mild, works in dichloromethane, and reliably stops at the aldehyde. Answer (D).

Hot copper: one reagent, three different answers

Pass the vapour of an alcohol over copper at 573 K and copper pulls off hydrogen (dehydrogenation) — or, for a tertiary alcohol, water:

$$\ce{RCH2OH ->[Cu][573\ K] RCHO + H2} \qquad \ce{R2CHOH ->[Cu][573\ K] R2C=O + H2}$$ $$\ce{(CH3)3COH ->[Cu][573\ K] (CH3)2C=CH2 + H2O}$$

Aldehyde, ketone or alkene: the product itself tells you whether the alcohol was 1°, 2° or 3°.

 The vapour of 2-methylpropan-2-ol is passed over heated copper at 573 K. The product is: 2-Methylpropanal 2-Methylpropene Propanone and methane No reaction

A tertiary alcohol has no hydrogen on the carbinol carbon, so it cannot be dehydrogenated to a carbonyl compound. Over hot copper it dehydrates instead: $\ce{(CH3)3COH -> (CH3)2C=CH2 + H2O}$. Answer (B).

The iodoform test: a yellow cloud for one special group

Warm a compound with iodine and sodium hydroxide. If it contains the group $\ce{CH3-CH(OH)-}$ (attached to H or to a carbon), a yellow precipitate of iodoform, $\ce{CHI3}$, appears, with a distinctive antiseptic smell.

Why that group? The hypoiodite ($\ce{NaOI}$) first oxidises $\ce{CH3CH(OH)R}$ to the methyl ketone $\ce{CH3COR}$. The three hydrogens of its $\ce{CH3}$ group are then replaced by iodine, and the $\ce{CI3}$ group is cleaved off as $\ce{CHI3}$:

Methanol has only one carbon; pentan-3-ol has no $\ce{CH3CH(OH)}$ unit; the tertiary alcohol cannot be oxidised to a methyl ketone; propanal is $\ce{CH3CH2CHO}$, not $\ce{CH3CHO}$.

Add the iodoform test for the special $\ce{CH3CH(OH)-}$ unit, and you can usually pin down an unknown alcohol completely. (The Victor Meyer test appears in older papers; the other three are the ones JEE Main asks about most.)

In the body, the enzyme alcohol dehydrogenase oxidises methanol to methanal and then to methanoic acid, which damages the optic nerve and can kill. A patient with methanol poisoning is given diluted ethanol intravenously: the enzyme is swamped with ethanol, methanol is oxidised only slowly, and the kidneys have time to excrete it unchanged.

$\ce{HIO4}$ cleaves the C–C bond between two neighbouring –OH groups, turning each carbon into a C=O: ethane-1,2-diol gives two molecules of methanal; glycerol gives two HCHO and one HCOOH. It occasionally appears in JEE Main as a structure-finding clue.

A pair is distinguished when exactly one member is positive. (A) ethanol +, methanol −. (B) propan-2-ol +, propan-1-ol −. (C) both have $\ce{CH3CH(OH)-}$: both positive, not distinguished. (D) pentan-2-ol +, pentan-3-ol −. Answer: A, B, D.
  • Oxidation removes H from O and from the carbinol C: 1° → aldehyde → acid; 2° → ketone; 3° resists. Strong: acidified $\ce{KMnO4}$, $\ce{K2Cr2O7}$ (orange → green). Stop at aldehyde: anhydrous $\ce{CrO3}$ or PCC.
  • Cu at 573 K: 1° → aldehyde, 2° → ketone, 3° → alkene.
  • Iodoform (yellow $\ce{CHI3}$) for $\ce{CH3CH(OH)-}$ and $\ce{CH3CO-}$: ethanol is the only positive 1° alcohol; methanol is negative.
  • Methanol → methanal → methanoic acid in the body; ethanol is the antidote.
