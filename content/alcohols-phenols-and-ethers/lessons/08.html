A ring with the volume turned up

Benzene is famously reluctant: to brominate it you need liquid bromine and an $\ce{FeBr3}$ catalyst. Drip bromine water into a solution of phenol and, in seconds, a white solid crashes out — three bromines on the ring, no catalyst, room temperature.

The reason is the oxygen’s lone pair. It is shared with the ring (+R effect), pushing electron density onto the ortho and para carbons — the same three carbons that carried the charge in phenoxide (Lesson 5). Electrophiles are drawn to them.

–OH is a strongly activating, ortho/para-directing group. Reactions need milder conditions than for benzene, and often go further than you want (polysubstitution) unless you tame them.

Nitration: dilute for one, concentrated for three

  • Dilute $\ce{HNO3}$ at 298 K gives a mixture of o-nitrophenol and p-nitrophenol. Steam distillation separates them: the ortho isomer, with its intramolecular hydrogen bond, is steam volatile; the para isomer stays behind (Lesson 4).
  • Concentrated $\ce{HNO3}$ gives 2,4,6-trinitrophenol — picric acid. But concentrated nitric acid also oxidises the electron-rich ring, so the yield is poor.

The industrial trick: first treat phenol with concentrated $\ce{H2SO4}$ to give phenol-2,4-disulphonic acid, then with concentrated $\ce{HNO3}$. The $\ce{-SO3H}$ groups protect the ring from oxidation and are then replaced by $\ce{-NO2}$ groups, giving picric acid cleanly.

Pick a reagent and watch where the electrophile lands. The glowing positions are the ones the –OH group activates; each new group slides in from the side.

Try these, in order:

  1. Compare Br₂ in CS₂, 273 K with Br₂ water. Same reagent, different solvent — why one bromine in one case and three in the other?
  2. Compare dil. HNO₃ with conc. HNO₃.
  3. Run Kolbe and Reimer–Tiemann. Both put a one-carbon group at the ortho position — what are the electrophiles?

Halogenation: the solvent decides

  • Bromine in a solvent of low polarity — $\ce{CS2}$ or $\ce{CHCl3}$ — at 273 K: monobromophenols, mainly p-bromophenol (with some ortho).
  • Bromine water: a white precipitate of 2,4,6-tribromophenol. This is a test for phenol.
$$\ce{C6H5OH + 3Br2 ->[H2O] C6H2Br3OH v + 3HBr}$$

Why the difference? In water, phenol partly ionises to phenoxide, which is even more strongly activating than phenol, and the polar solvent helps polarise $\ce{Br2}$ into a ready electrophile. In $\ce{CS2}$ at low temperature, phenol stays un-ionised and bromine stays only weakly polarised: the reaction is slow enough to stop after one bromine.

An answer option that brominates phenol with $\ce{Br2/FeBr3}$ is a decoy. The ring is so activated that a Lewis acid is never needed — and with water as solvent you cannot even stop at one bromine.

 The conversion of phenol into 2,4,6-tribromophenol can be carried out with: $\ce{Br2}$ in $\ce{CS2}$ at 273 K Bromine water $\ce{Br2}$ in $\ce{CHCl3}$ at 273 K $\ce{Br2}$ with $\ce{FeBr3}$, in the dark

Only in water does phenol (partly as phenoxide) react three times, at all the activated o- and p-positions, giving the white precipitate of 2,4,6-tribromophenol. Low-polarity solvents at 273 K give monobromophenols, and a Lewis acid is not what makes the difference. Answer (B).

Sulphonation and the temperature switch

Concentrated $\ce{H2SO4}$ sulphonates phenol. Near room temperature the ortho product (o-hydroxybenzenesulphonic acid) forms fastest; at about 373 K the more stable para isomer dominates. Sulphonation is reversible, so given heat and time the ring settles into its most stable arrangement.

 Phenol is heated with concentrated $\ce{H2SO4}$ at 373 K. The major product is: Benzenesulphonic acid m-Hydroxybenzenesulphonic acid o-Hydroxybenzenesulphonic acid p-Hydroxybenzenesulphonic acid

–OH directs to ortho and para. Sulphonation is reversible: at the higher temperature the reaction reaches equilibrium and the less crowded, more stable para isomer dominates. (At room temperature, the faster-forming ortho isomer is the major product.) Answer (D).

Kolbe’s reaction: from phenol to aspirin

Phenoxide is so electron-rich that it attacks even carbon dioxide, a weak electrophile. Heat sodium phenoxide with $\ce{CO2}$ (about 400 K, 4–7 atm), then acidify, and you get salicylic acid (2-hydroxybenzoic acid), mainly at the ortho position:

$$\ce{C6H5ONa ->[(i) CO2, 400 K, 4–7 atm][(ii) H+]} \text{2-hydroxybenzoic acid (salicylic acid)}$$

Acetylate its –OH with ethanoic anhydride and you have aspirin. (Plain phenol is not reactive enough for $\ce{CO2}$ — the base has to make phenoxide first.)

 The major product obtained on interaction of phenol with sodium hydroxide and carbon dioxide is: Salicylaldehyde Salicylic acid Phthalic acid Benzoic acid

NaOH turns phenol into the strongly nucleophilic phenoxide, which attacks $\ce{CO2}$ at the ortho position (Kolbe’s reaction). Acidification gives 2-hydroxybenzoic acid — salicylic acid. Answer (B).

 Sodium phenoxide, when heated with $\ce{CO2}$ under pressure at 125 °C, yields a product which on acetylation produces C. The major product C would be: Acetylsalicylic acid (aspirin) Phenyl ethanoate Methyl salicylate 2-Hydroxyacetophenone

Kolbe’s reaction gives (sodium) salicylate → salicylic acid. Acetylation puts $\ce{CH3CO-}$ on the phenolic –OH: acetylsalicylic acid, aspirin. Answer (A).

Reimer–Tiemann: an aldehyde from chloroform

Warm phenol with chloroform and aqueous sodium hydroxide (about 340 K), then acidify, and a –CHO group appears at the ortho position: salicylaldehyde (2-hydroxybenzaldehyde).

Hydroxide pulls a proton off chloroform; the $\ce{CCl3-}$ ion then loses $\ce{Cl-}$ to give dichlorocarbene, $\ce{:CCl2}$ — a neutral carbon with only six valence electrons, hungry for a pair:

$$\ce{CHCl3 + OH- -> CCl3^- + H2O} \qquad \ce{CCl3^- -> :CCl2 + Cl-}$$

The phenoxide ring attacks $\ce{:CCl2}$ at the ortho carbon. After proton shuffles, the ring carries a $\ce{-CHCl2}$ group (a substituted benzal chloride). Aqueous alkali hydrolyses $\ce{-CHCl2}$ to –CHO, and acid releases salicylaldehyde.

Swap chloroform for carbon tetrachloride and the same route ends in a –COOH: salicylic acid.

 In the Reimer–Tiemann reaction of phenol with $\ce{CHCl3}$ and aqueous NaOH, the electrophile that attacks the ring is: The dichloromethyl cation, $\ce{^+CHCl2}$ Dichlorocarbene, $\ce{:CCl2}$ The trichloromethyl anion, $\ce{^-CCl3}$ The formyl cation, $\ce{HC#O+}$

Base converts $\ce{CHCl3}$ to $\ce{CCl3-}$, which loses chloride to give dichlorocarbene. Its carbon has only a sextet of electrons, so it is electrophilic. ($\ce{CCl3-}$ is its precursor and is a nucleophile, not an electrophile.) Answer (B).

Three more reactions and a colour test

  • Zinc dust: heating phenol with zinc dust removes the oxygen: $\ce{C6H5OH + Zn -> C6H6 + ZnO}$.
  • Oxidation: chromic acid ($\ce{Na2Cr2O7/H2SO4}$) oxidises phenol to benzoquinone, a yellow conjugated diketone. Even air slowly oxidises phenol, which is why old samples turn pink or dark.
  • Ferric chloride test: neutral $\ce{FeCl3}$ gives a violet colour with phenol (a coloured iron–phenoxide complex). Most phenols and enols give coloured complexes; alcohols and ethers do not.

Taming the ring. Converting the –OH into an ester (for example with methyl chloroformate, $\ce{ClCOOCH3}$, in NaOH) ties up the oxygen’s lone pair with a carbonyl group. The ring is still ortho/para directed, but much less activated — so it can now be brominated just once, mostly at the less crowded para position.

Heating an aryl ester such as phenyl ethanoate with $\ce{AlCl3}$ moves the acyl group from oxygen onto the ring, giving o- and p-hydroxyacetophenone (more para at low temperature, more ortho at high temperature). It appears now and then in JEE Main conversion questions.

 Phenol reacts with methyl chloroformate, $\ce{ClCOOCH3}$, in the presence of NaOH to form product A. A reacts with $\ce{Br2}$ to form product B. A and B are respectively: $\ce{C6H5-O-COOCH3}$ and 4-bromophenyl methyl carbonate $\ce{C6H5-O-COOCH3}$ and 2,4,6-tribromophenyl methyl carbonate Methyl salicylate and its 5-bromo derivative Anisole and p-bromoanisole

NaOH makes phenoxide, which attacks the C=O of methyl chloroformate and displaces chloride: A = methyl phenyl carbonate, $\ce{C6H5OCOOCH3}$. The –O–COOCH₃ group is still ortho/para directing, but its oxygen lone pair is shared with the carbonyl, so the ring is only mildly activated and takes one bromine, at the less hindered para position. Answer (A).

 Which of these give a violet (or similar) colour with neutral $\ce{FeCl3}$ solution? Phenol Benzyl alcohol Salicylic acid Anisole

The test needs a free phenolic –OH on the ring. Phenol and salicylic acid have one. Benzyl alcohol’s –OH is on a $\ce{CH2}$, not on the ring; anisole’s oxygen carries a methyl, not an H. Answer: A, C.
  • –OH: strongly activating, o/p-directing. Mild conditions, easy polysubstitution.
  • Nitration: dil. $\ce{HNO3}$, 298 K → o- + p-nitrophenol (separate by steam distillation); conc. $\ce{HNO3}$ → picric acid (better via sulphonation first).
  • Bromination: $\ce{Br2/CS2}$ (or $\ce{CHCl3}$), 273 K → mainly p-bromophenol; bromine water → 2,4,6-tribromophenol (white ppt). No Lewis acid.
  • Sulphonation: ortho near room temperature, para at 373 K.
  • Kolbe: phenoxide + $\ce{CO2}$ → salicylic acid (→ aspirin). Reimer–Tiemann: $\ce{CHCl3}$/NaOH, via $\ce{:CCl2}$ → salicylaldehyde.
  • Zn dust → benzene; chromic acid → benzoquinone; $\ce{FeCl3}$ → violet.
