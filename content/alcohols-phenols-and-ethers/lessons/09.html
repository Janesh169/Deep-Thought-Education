“Gentlemen, this is no humbug”

On 16 October 1846, in a steep-sided operating theatre in Boston now called the Ether Dome, William Morton held a glass inhaler to the mouth of a young man named Gilbert Abbott. Abbott breathed diethyl ether vapour and fell asleep. The surgeon, John Collins Warren, removed a tumour from his neck while the audience waited for a scream that never came. Warren turned to them and said: “Gentlemen, this is no humbug.”

Ether was used as an anaesthetic for over a century, until safer, non-flammable gases replaced it. Its other career has never ended: it is one of chemistry’s favourite solvents, precisely because it is so unreactive. This lesson is about how ethers are made, and about the one reagent that finally breaks them.

Making ethers, method 1: two alcohols, one water

Heat ethanol with concentrated sulphuric acid at 413 K and two molecules combine, losing one water:

$$\ce{2CH3CH2OH ->[conc.\ H2SO4][413\ K] CH3CH2-O-CH2CH3 + H2O}$$

The mechanism is substitution, not elimination. The acid protonates one alcohol molecule; a second, un-protonated alcohol molecule attacks its carbon with an oxygen lone pair and pushes out water ($\mathrm{S_N2}$); loss of a proton gives the ether.

Same reagents, about 30 degrees apart: 413 K → diethyl ether, 443 K → ethene (Lesson 6). And the method only works well for primary alcohols making symmetrical ethers: 2° and 3° alcohols simply dehydrate to alkenes, and two different alcohols give a messy mixture of three ethers.

 Ethanol is heated with concentrated $\ce{H2SO4}$ at 413 K. The main organic product is: Ethene Ethoxyethane Ethyl hydrogen sulphate only Ethanal

At 413 K a protonated ethanol molecule is attacked by a second ethanol molecule ($\mathrm{S_N2}$), giving diethyl ether (ethoxyethane). At 443 K elimination takes over and ethene is the product. Answer (B).

Method 2: Williamson synthesis

The general way to make any ether, symmetrical or not: an alkyl halide plus a sodium alkoxide.

$$\ce{R-X + R'-O^-Na+ -> R-O-R' + NaX}$$

The alkoxide ion is the nucleophile and attacks the carbon of the alkyl halide from the back, pushing out the halide: an $\mathrm{S_N2}$ reaction. So it works best when the halide carbon is uncrowded — methyl or primary.

The catch is that an alkoxide is also a strong base. Give it a crowded tertiary halide and, unable to reach the carbon, it grabs a β-hydrogen instead: elimination, giving an alkene. Secondary halides give a mixture.

Put the bulky group on the alkoxide and the small group on the halide. For aryl ethers, the aryl group must be on the oxygen: $\ce{C6H5O^-Na+ + CH3I -> C6H5OCH3}$ (anisole) works, but $\ce{C6H5Br + CH3O^-Na+}$ does not — aryl halides do not do $\mathrm{S_N2}$.

 2-Chloro-2-methylpentane on reaction with sodium methoxide in methanol yields: (I) $\ce{CH3CH2CH2C(CH3)2OCH3}$ (II) $\ce{CH3CH2CH2C(CH3)=CH2}$ (III) $\ce{CH3CH2CH=C(CH3)2}$ I and III only III only I and II only All of these

The halide is tertiary. Substitution can only happen through the carbocation ($\mathrm{S_N1}$, with methanol or methoxide capturing it) and gives the ether I. Methoxide is also a strong base, so elimination competes, removing a β-H from either side to give both alkenes, II and III (III, the more substituted, is the larger share). All three products form. Answer (D).

In D the halide is tertiary: ethoxide acts as a base and eliminates HBr, giving 2-methylpropene as the major product. To make tert-butyl ethyl ether, use sodium tert-butoxide with bromoethane. Breaking an ether: only HI and HBr will do

Ethers ignore bases, dilute acids, oxidants and reductants — the reason they make good solvents. But heated with a concentrated hydrogen halide, the C–O bond breaks:

$$\ce{R-O-R + HX -> R-X + R-OH} \qquad \text{(excess HX: } \ce{R-OH -> R-X}\text{ too)}$$

The order is HI > HBr > HCl: iodide is the best nucleophile, and HI the strongest acid. Two steps:

  1. Protonation of the ether oxygen, making a good leaving group (a neutral alcohol).
  2. Attack by $\ce{X-}$, which decides which side breaks:
    • If both groups are methyl or primary: $\mathrm{S_N2}$, so iodide attacks the less hindered carbon. $\ce{CH3-O-CH2CH3 + HI -> CH3I + CH3CH2OH}$.
    • If one group is tertiary: the C–O bond to it breaks on its own to give the stable 3° carbocation ($\mathrm{S_N1}$), which iodide then captures. $\ce{(CH3)3C-O-CH3 + HI -> (CH3)3C-I + CH3OH}$.

Aryl alkyl ethers always break on the alkyl side: $\ce{C6H5-O-CH3 + HI -> C6H5OH + CH3I}$. The O–aryl bond has partial double-bond character, and iodide cannot do $\mathrm{S_N2}$ on an sp² carbon or make an aryl cation. And even excess HI does not turn the phenol into iodobenzene, for the same reason.

The protonated ether can lose methanol to form the stable tertiary carbocation ($\mathrm{S_N1}$), which iodide captures: $\ce{(CH3)3CI + CH3OH}$. This is the opposite of what $\mathrm{S_N2}$ at the less hindered methyl group would predict — the classic exam twist. Answer (B).

The ring of an aromatic ether

In anisole the oxygen’s lone pair is shared with the ring, just as in phenol, so –OCH₃ is activating and ortho/para directing (somewhat less than –OH). Typical reactions:

  • Bromination: bromine in ethanoic acid, with no catalyst → mainly p-bromoanisole (plus some ortho).
  • Friedel–Crafts alkylation: $\ce{CH3Cl/AlCl3}$ → 2- and 4-methoxytoluene.
  • Friedel–Crafts acylation: $\ce{CH3COCl/AlCl3}$ → 2- and 4-methoxyacetophenone.
  • Nitration: conc. $\ce{H2SO4}$ + conc. $\ce{HNO3}$ → 2- and 4-nitroanisole.

Para is usually the major product: the ortho positions sit right next to the methoxy group and are more crowded.

Left in air and light, ethers slowly react with oxygen to form peroxides, which can explode when the ether is distilled to dryness. That is why ether comes in dark bottles, is used fresh, and is tested for peroxides before distillation — a lesson some labs learned the hard way.
