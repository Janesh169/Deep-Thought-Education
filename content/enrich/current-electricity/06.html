What the electricity bill is really charging for

Look at an electricity bill. It does not charge you for amperes, and it does not charge you for volts. It charges for units — and one unit is one kilowatt-hour: a kilowatt of power for one hour.

$$1\ \text{kWh} = 1000\ \text{W} \times 3600\ \text{s} = 3.6 \times 10^{6}\ \text{J}$$

A 2 kW geyser left on for half an hour uses exactly one unit. A 10 W LED bulb takes a hundred hours to do the same.

So what you pay for is energy. This lesson follows that energy as it leaves the circuit — as heat, light and motion, and, when someone is careless, as melted wire.

Two bulbs in series: the one with the smaller wattage rating glows brighter — it has the larger resistance, and the current through both is the same. In parallel the order flips back: the higher-rated bulb wins, because the voltage is the same and its resistance is smaller.
