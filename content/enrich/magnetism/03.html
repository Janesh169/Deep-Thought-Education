A race in Paris, autumn 1820

News of Ørsted’s twitching compass reached Paris in September 1820, and the city’s physicists dropped everything.

Two of them, Jean-Baptiste Biot and Félix Savart, hung a small magnetised needle near a long straight wire and let it swing. The stronger the field, the faster it oscillated — so by timing the swings at different distances from the wire, they could measure the field. Their numbers said it fell off as $1/r$. They announced the result before the end of October.

Laplace then asked the next question: if a whole wire gives $1/r$, what does each tiny piece of wire contribute? The answer is the law in this lesson — the magnetic twin of Coulomb’s law.

Long straight wire: $\dfrac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi r}$ — the field is spread round a whole circle, so $2\pi r$ sits downstairs. Centre of a circular loop: $\dfrac{\mu_0 I}{2R}$ — no $\pi$. An arc of angle $\theta$: the loop’s value times $\dfrac{\theta}{2\pi}$.

Let your shoulders drop, unclench your jaw, and take three slow breaths.
