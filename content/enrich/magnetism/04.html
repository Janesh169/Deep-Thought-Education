One week in September

On 11 September 1820, André-Marie Ampère watched a demonstration of Ørsted’s experiment in Paris. One week later he was back before the Academy of Sciences with something new: two parallel wires carrying currents pull on each other — no magnet involved at all.

Over the next few years he worked out how currents make fields so thoroughly that James Clerk Maxwell later called him “the Newton of electricity”.

One of his results is the shortcut in this lesson. When the currents are arranged symmetrically — a long wire, a solenoid, a toroid — you can find the field without doing a single integral.

Inside a long solenoid: $B = \mu_0 n I$, where $n$ is the number of turns per metre — not the total number of turns. At the very end of the solenoid: half that. Outside: almost nothing.

Stand up, stretch your arms overhead, and sit back down.

Then: the solenoid — a bottle of perfectly uniform field, and the reason an MRI scanner is shaped like a tunnel.
