The lamp in the cathedral

The story goes that in 1583 a bored nineteen-year-old named Galileo Galilei sat in the cathedral at Pisa watching a hanging lamp swing in a draught. He timed it against his own pulse and noticed something strange. When the swings were wide the lamp moved fast, and when they were narrow it moved slowly, but each swing took the same time.

That one observation led to the pendulum clock. The same steady beat runs through the rest of the world too:

  • the quartz crystal in a wristwatch vibrates exactly 32 768 times a second;
  • your heart beats roughly once a second;
  • the sea rises and falls about every 12 hours 25 minutes;
  • the atoms in a solid jiggle about 10¹³ times a second.

This chapter is about the one equation all of these share. By the end you will be able to look at a spring, a pendulum, a floating block or a tunnel through the Earth and write down its period in a single line.

Every lesson has at least one live experiment. Drag things, break things, and check your guesses. Lessons 1–2 build the toolkit, 3–6 apply it to real systems, 7 adds friction and pushing, and 8 is a game and a final round of past papers. Each lesson also has a short breather in the middle. Take it.

Periodic and oscillatory

A motion is periodic if it repeats itself exactly after a fixed time. That time is the period, $T$. The Moon going round the Earth is periodic, and so are the hands of a clock.

A motion is oscillatory if, on top of repeating, it goes to and fro about a fixed middle point, called the mean position or equilibrium position. A swing does this, and so does a guitar string. Every oscillation is periodic, but not every periodic motion is an oscillation: the Moon never comes back through a middle point.

Three numbers describe the rhythm:

QuantityMeaningUnit
Period $T$time for one complete cycles
Frequency $f = \dfrac{1}{T}$cycles per secondHz (s⁻¹)
Angular frequency $\omega = \dfrac{2\pi}{T} = 2\pi f$radians of “phase” per second (more on phase soon)rad s⁻¹

A wristwatch crystal has $f = 32\,768\ \text{Hz}$, so $T \approx 30.5\ \mu\text{s}$. A tide has $T \approx 12.4\ \text{h}$. The physics is the same, only the clock runs at a different speed.

Functions that repeat

The natural language of periodic motion is the sine and the cosine. $\sin\theta$ repeats every $2\pi$, so $\sin\omega t$ repeats when $\omega t$ grows by $2\pi$. That gives the rule:

$$\sin\omega t \text{ and } \cos\omega t \text{ have period } T = \frac{2\pi}{\omega}.$$

A few more facts are worth knowing, because JEE likes to test them:

  • Squares halve the period. $\sin^2\omega t = \tfrac{1}{2}(1 - \cos 2\omega t)$, so it repeats every $\pi/\omega$.
  • Sums repeat on the LCM. $\sin\omega t + \sin 2\omega t$ repeats when both parts do. The periods are $2\pi/\omega$ and $\pi/\omega$, so the sum repeats every $2\pi/\omega$. It is periodic, but as you will see it is not SHM.
  • Same frequency, still a sine. $a\sin\omega t + b\cos\omega t = \sqrt{a^2+b^2}\,\sin(\omega t + \alpha)$. Adding a sine and a cosine of the same $\omega$ gives one sine wave with a bigger amplitude (Lesson 2).
  • Functions such as $e^{-t}$, $\log t$ or $t^2$ never repeat, so they are not periodic.
 What is the period of $x = 3\sin(2\pi t) + 4\sin(3\pi t)$, with $t$ in seconds? $1\ \text{s}$ $\tfrac{2}{3}\ \text{s}$ $2\ \text{s}$ $\tfrac{1}{3}\ \text{s}$ Find each part’s period, then the smallest time that is a whole number of both.

The first part has $T_1 = 2\pi/2\pi = 1\ \text{s}$ and the second has $T_2 = 2\pi/3\pi = \tfrac{2}{3}\ \text{s}$. The sum repeats only when both parts do, which happens at the least common multiple: $2\ \text{s}$ is 2 periods of the first and 3 of the second. Answer: 2 s.

 A particle moves along a line with $x = \sin^2\omega t$. Which statement is correct? It is SHM about $x = 0$ with period $2\pi/\omega$ It is SHM about $x = \tfrac{1}{2}$ with period $\pi/\omega$ It is periodic but not SHM, with period $\pi/\omega$ It is not periodic Rewrite $\sin^2\omega t$ using the double-angle formula, then differentiate twice.

$x = \tfrac{1}{2} - \tfrac{1}{2}\cos 2\omega t$. That is a cosine of angular frequency $2\omega$ (period $\pi/\omega$) about the point $x = \tfrac{1}{2}$. Differentiating twice gives

$$a = 2\omega^2\cos 2\omega t = -4\omega^2\left(x - \tfrac{1}{2}\right),$$

which is exactly the SHM condition with mean position $x = \tfrac12$. Answer: B.

Exam note: a version of this question appeared in AIEEE 2005, and the official key said “periodic but not simple harmonic”. The physics says otherwise, because $a = -4\omega^2(x - \tfrac12)$. If it comes up again, check whether the options mention a shifted mean position.

What makes it “simple harmonic”

Pull a block on a spring to one side and let go. The spring pulls it back towards the middle. It overshoots, gets pulled back again, and keeps going. The important thing is how hard the spring pulls: twice as far out means twice the pull. That is Hooke’s law, $F = -kx$.

Any motion where the restoring force is proportional to the displacement and points back towards the mean position is called simple harmonic motion (SHM):

$$F = -kx \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad a = -\omega^2 x, \qquad \omega^2 = \frac{k}{m}.$$

The minus sign says the force always points back to the middle. The fact that $x$ appears to the first power, with no $x^2$ or $x^3$, is what makes it simple.

If you can write $a = -(\text{positive constant}) \times (\text{displacement from some fixed point})$, the motion is SHM, and that constant is $\omega^2$. For rotation, the same test reads $\alpha = -\omega^2\theta$, which is angular SHM.

The big idea: SHM is a shadow

Here is the picture that makes the rest of the chapter easy. Take a point moving round a circle of radius $A$ at a steady angular speed $\omega$. Shine a light from the side and look at its shadow on a wall. The shadow slides up and down a straight line, and that up-and-down motion is exactly SHM.

If the point is at angle $\theta = \omega t + \phi$ from the horizontal, its height is $A\sin\theta$, so the shadow is at

$$x = A\sin(\omega t + \phi).$$

This circle is called the reference circle (or auxiliary circle). Try it below. Pause it, drag the point to any angle, and turn on the arrows.

Things to try. (1) Turn on the arrows. The point’s speed $A\omega$ never changes, yet the shadow’s velocity (green) is biggest at the middle and zero at the ends. (2) The point’s acceleration $A\omega^2$ always points to the centre. Its shadow (yellow) is biggest at the ends and always points back to the middle: that is $a = -\omega^2 x$. (3) Double $\omega$ and watch the curve on the right squeeze to half its period.

Watch the ring. Breathe in while it grows and out while it shrinks. Its rhythm is about the same as a slow ocean swell, roughly 9 seconds a cycle. Three breaths, then on to the last idea.

What the circle gives you for free

The shadow can never go faster than the point itself, which moves at $A\omega$. It can never accelerate harder than the point’s centripetal acceleration, $A\omega^2$. Both limits are reached:

  • Top speed $v_{\max} = A\omega$, when the point is at the top or bottom of the circle and the shadow is passing through the mean position.
  • Largest acceleration $a_{\max} = A\omega^2$, when the shadow is at an extreme ($x = \pm A$) and has stopped for an instant.

Keep this circle in mind. In the next lesson it gives you the velocity, the acceleration, the phase and the time between any two positions, with almost no algebra.

Periodic means it repeats. Oscillatory means it repeats back and forth through a mean position. $T = 1/f = 2\pi/\omega$. SHM means $a = -\omega^2 x$, and it is the shadow of a point going steadily round a circle.
