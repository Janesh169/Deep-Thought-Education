Reading the equation

Every SHM can be written as

$$x = A\sin(\omega t + \phi).$$
SymbolNameWhat it does
$A$Amplitudethe largest displacement. $x$ stays between $-A$ and $+A$.
$\omega$Angular frequencyhow fast the phase grows: $\omega = 2\pi/T$.
$\omega t + \phi$Phasethe angle on the reference circle. It tells you where the body is and which way it is going.
$\phi$Initial phase (epoch)the phase at $t = 0$, which sets where the clock starts.

Using $\cos$ instead of $\sin$ is just as correct, because $\cos\theta = \sin(\theta + \pi/2)$. Switching only changes $\phi$ by $\pi/2$. Use whichever makes $\phi$ simplest: $\cos$ if the motion starts at an extreme, and $\sin$ if it starts at the mean position.

Velocity and acceleration

Differentiate once and then once more:

$$v = \frac{dx}{dt} = A\omega\cos(\omega t + \phi), \qquad a = \frac{dv}{dt} = -A\omega^2\sin(\omega t + \phi) = -\omega^2 x.$$

It also helps to have $v$ in terms of position instead of time. Since $\sin^2 + \cos^2 = 1$:

$$v = \pm\,\omega\sqrt{A^2 - x^2}.$$

From $x = A\sin(\omega t+\phi)$ we get $\sin(\omega t + \phi) = x/A$, so $\cos(\omega t + \phi) = \pm\sqrt{1 - x^2/A^2}$. Then $v = A\omega\cos(\omega t + \phi) = \pm\omega\sqrt{A^2 - x^2}$. The sign depends on which way the body is moving, because the same $x$ is passed twice in every cycle, once in each direction.

$\cos$ runs a quarter-cycle ahead of $\sin$, so $v$ leads $x$ by $\pi/2$: velocity peaks a quarter-period before displacement does. $a = -\omega^2 x$ is $x$ flipped over, so $a$ leads $x$ by $\pi$, meaning they are always opposite. Watch the three graphs march in step below.

(2) Move $\phi$ in steps of 90°: the graphs slide sideways, but their shapes and their order never change. (3) Halve the period and read the $v$ and $a$ values off the readouts. $v_{\max}$ doubles and $a_{\max}$ goes up four times, because $v_{\max} = A\omega$ and $a_{\max} = A\omega^2$.

Finding the initial phase

Two facts at $t = 0$ fix $\phi$: where the body is and which way it is moving. On the reference circle, a body moving towards $+x$ is on the right half of the circle ($\cos\phi > 0$), and one moving towards $-x$ is on the left half.

  • Starting at the mean position, moving towards $+A$: $\phi = 0$.
  • Starting at $+A$, at rest: $\phi = \pi/2$, so $x = A\cos\omega t$.
  • Starting at $x = A/2$, moving towards $+A$: $\sin\phi = \tfrac12$ with $\cos\phi > 0$, so $\phi = \pi/6$.
  • Starting at $x = A/2$, moving back towards the mean: $\sin\phi = \tfrac12$ with $\cos\phi < 0$, so $\phi = 5\pi/6$.

$\sin\phi = \tfrac12$ is satisfied by both $\pi/6$ and $5\pi/6$. A calculator only gives you the first. Always use the direction of motion to choose between them.

 A particle in SHM, $x = A\sin(\omega t + \phi)$, is at $x = -A/2$ at $t = 0$ and is moving towards the mean position. Then $\phi$ is $\pi/6$ $-\pi/6$ $7\pi/6$ $5\pi/6$ $\sin\phi = -\tfrac12$. Moving towards the mean from the negative side means moving in the $+x$ direction, so $v > 0$ and $\cos\phi > 0$.

$\sin\phi = -\tfrac12$ gives $\phi = -\pi/6$ or $7\pi/6$. Moving towards the mean from $x = -A/2$ means moving in the $+x$ direction, so $v = A\omega\cos\phi > 0$, which needs $\cos\phi > 0$. That picks $\phi = -\pi/6$. ($7\pi/6$ would mean the particle is heading out towards $-A$.) Answer: B.

Timing any part of a swing

To find how long the body takes to go from one position to another, don’t solve sine equations. Find the angle the point sweeps on the reference circle and divide by $\omega$:

$$t = \frac{\Delta\theta}{\omega} = \frac{\Delta\theta}{2\pi}\,T.$$

$0 \to A/2$30°$T/12$

$0 \to \sqrt3A/2$60°$T/6$
$0 \to A$90°$T/4$
$A/2 \to A$60°$T/6$

Going from $0$ to $A/2$ takes $T/12$, but going from $A/2$ to $A$ takes twice as long, $T/6$. The body is slow near the ends. That is also why a pendulum spends most of its time near its turning points.

 A body moves in SHM with period 2.4 s. How long, in seconds, does it take to go directly from $x = A/2$ to $x = A$? On the reference circle that is a 60° sweep.

The angle on the circle goes from 30° to 90°, a sweep of 60°, which is $\tfrac16$ of a turn. So $t = T/6 = 2.4/6 = 0.4\ \text{s}$.

 A particle moves in SHM along a straight line. Starting from rest, it covers a distance $a$ in the first $\tau$ seconds and a further $2a$ in the next $\tau$ seconds, in the same direction. Then amplitude $= 3a$, period $= 6\tau$ amplitude $= 2a$, period $= 6\tau$ amplitude $= 2a$, period $= 8\tau$ amplitude $= 3a/2$, period $= 4\tau$ “Starting from rest” means it starts at an extreme, so the distance covered after time $t$ is $A(1 - \cos\omega t)$.

From the extreme, the distance covered is $A(1 - \cos\omega t)$. Let $c = \cos\omega\tau$:

$$A(1 - c) = a, \qquad A(1 - \cos 2\omega\tau) = 3a \;\Rightarrow\; 2A(1 - c^2) = 3a.$$

Dividing, $2(1 + c) = 3$, so $c = \tfrac12$. That gives $\omega\tau = \pi/3$, so $T = 2\pi/\omega = 6\tau$, and $A = a/(1 - \tfrac12) = 2a$. Answer: B. On the circle: 60° per $\tau$, and $x$ goes from $A$ to $A/2$ to $-A/2$.

 A particle performs SHM with amplitude $A$. Its speed is suddenly trebled at the instant it is $2A/3$ from the mean position. The new amplitude is $3A$ $A\sqrt3$ $7A/3$ $\dfrac{A}{3}\sqrt{41}$ The spring and mass are unchanged, so $\omega$ is unchanged. Use $A^2 = x^2 + v^2/\omega^2$ before and after.

Before: $v^2 = \omega^2(A^2 - \tfrac49A^2) = \tfrac59\omega^2A^2$. After trebling: $v'^2 = 9v^2 = 5\omega^2A^2$. The position is the same and $\omega$ is the same, so

$$A'^2 = x^2 + \frac{v'^2}{\omega^2} = \frac49A^2 + 5A^2 = \frac{49}{9}A^2 \;\Rightarrow\; A' = \frac{7A}{3}.$$

A sine plus a cosine

If a displacement comes as $x = a\sin\omega t + b\cos\omega t$, it is still SHM, because both terms share the same $\omega$. Rewrite it as one sine:

$$x = R\sin(\omega t + \alpha), \qquad R = \sqrt{a^2 + b^2}, \quad \tan\alpha = \frac{b}{a}.$$

Worked example (AIEEE 2003): $x = 4(\cos\pi t + \sin\pi t)$. Here $a = b = 4$, so the amplitude is $\sqrt{16 + 16} = 4\sqrt2$, and $\omega = \pi$ gives $T = 2\ \text{s}$.

 An SHM is described by $y = 5(\sin 3\pi t + \sqrt3\cos 3\pi t)$ cm. Its amplitude and period are 10 cm and 3/2 s 5 cm and 2/3 s 5 cm and 3/2 s 10 cm and 2/3 s $R = \sqrt{a^2 + b^2}$ with $a = 5$, $b = 5\sqrt3$.

$R = 5\sqrt{1 + 3} = 10\ \text{cm}$, and $\omega = 3\pi$ gives $T = 2\pi/3\pi = 2/3\ \text{s}$. Answer: D.

 Two particles move in SHM with the same amplitude $A$ and the same angular frequency along the x-axis. Their mean positions are a distance $X_0$ apart, with $X_0 > A$. If the greatest separation between them is $X_0 + A$, the phase difference between their motions is $\pi/2$ $\pi/3$ $\pi/4$ $\pi/6$ The separation is $X_0 + A\sin(\omega t + \delta) - A\sin\omega t$. What is the largest value of a difference of two sines?

$x_2 - x_1 = X_0 + A[\sin(\omega t + \delta) - \sin\omega t] = X_0 + 2A\sin\tfrac{\delta}{2}\cos(\omega t + \tfrac{\delta}{2})$. Its maximum is $X_0 + 2A\sin\tfrac{\delta}{2}$. Setting this equal to $X_0 + A$ gives $\sin\tfrac{\delta}{2} = \tfrac12$, so $\delta = \pi/3$. Answer: B.
