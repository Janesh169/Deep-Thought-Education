The trampoline trade

At the top of a bounce on a trampoline you hang still for an instant: no speed, and all the energy stored in your height. As you sink into the mat, that energy goes into the stretched springs. In between, you are moving at full speed. An oscillator works the same way: it keeps trading the same energy back and forth between motion and storage.

For a spring, the stored energy is the work done in stretching it, and the energy of motion is the usual $\tfrac12mv^2$:

$$U = \tfrac12kx^2, \qquad K = \tfrac12mv^2 = \tfrac12m\omega^2(A^2 - x^2) = \tfrac12k(A^2 - x^2).$$

Add them and every $x$ cancels:

$$E = K + U = \tfrac12kA^2 = \tfrac12m\omega^2A^2.$$

$E = \tfrac12 m\omega^2A^2$ is constant and grows as the square of the amplitude. Double the amplitude and you store four times the energy.

Energy against position

The potential energy is a bowl. The kinetic energy is the gap between the bowl and the flat total. They are equal where $\tfrac12kx^2 = \tfrac14kA^2$, which is at $x = \pm A/\sqrt2 \approx \pm 0.71A$, not at $A/2$.

 A body moves in SHM. Its potential energy (PE), kinetic energy (KE) and total energy (TE) are measured as functions of displacement $x$. Which statement is true? KE is maximum when $x = 0$ TE is zero when $x = 0$ KE is maximum when $x$ is maximum PE is maximum when $x = 0$

$K = \tfrac12k(A^2 - x^2)$ is largest at $x = 0$, the mean position, where the speed is $A\omega$. The total energy is never zero, and PE is zero at $x = 0$. Answer: A.

 At what displacement, as a fraction of the amplitude ($x/A$), is the kinetic energy of an SHM three times its potential energy? If $K = 3U$, then $E = 4U$.

$E = K + U = 4U$, so $\tfrac12kA^2 = 4\cdot\tfrac12kx^2$, which gives $x = A/2$. Answer: 0.5.

Energy against time

With $x = A\sin\omega t$, the two energies are

$$U = E\sin^2\omega t, \qquad K = E\cos^2\omega t.$$

Because of the squares, each energy oscillates at twice the frequency of the motion, with period $T/2$. Over a whole cycle each one averages $E/2$: on average, the energy is shared equally between motion and storage.

Things to try. (1) Watch the aqua (kinetic) and coral (potential) bars. They swap twice per oscillation, and the time graph below shows the doubled frequency. (2) Drag the ball to a new amplitude. Total energy grows as $A^2$, but the period doesn’t change. (3) Turn on Stiffening spring: the bowl gets steeper walls ($U = \tfrac12kx^2 + \tfrac14bx^4$). Now a bigger swing has a shorter period. The motion is still periodic, but it isn’t SHM any more.

Starting at the mean, $x = A\sin\omega t$ and $v = A\omega\cos\omega t$, so $K = E\cos^2\omega t$. That curve starts at $E$, reaches zero at $T/4$ (the extreme), is back to $E$ at $T/2$ and is never negative. Answer: A.

Every valley is a spring

Pick any smooth potential energy curve $U(x)$ with a minimum at $x_0$. Close to the bottom, every smooth valley looks like a parabola. The Taylor series makes this exact:

$$U(x) \approx U(x_0) + \tfrac12U''(x_0)\,(x - x_0)^2 + \dots$$

The first-derivative term is missing because the slope is zero at a minimum. So for small displacements the body feels an effective spring constant $k = U''(x_0)$, and it moves in SHM with

$$\omega = \sqrt{\frac{U''(x_0)}{m}}.$$

That is why SHM is everywhere: atoms in molecules, a marble rolling in a bowl, a ship rocking at sea. All of them are near the bottom of some valley. It also tells you when SHM fails: large amplitudes reach the parts of the valley that aren’t parabolic, as you saw with the stiffening spring.

 A particle of mass $m$ moves in a potential $U(x) = U_0(1 - \cos\alpha x)$. Its angular frequency for small oscillations about $x = 0$ is $\sqrt{U_0/m}$ $\alpha\sqrt{m/U_0}$ $\alpha\sqrt{U_0/m}$ $\alpha^2\sqrt{U_0/m}$ $k = U''(0)$.

$U' = U_0\alpha\sin\alpha x$ and $U'' = U_0\alpha^2\cos\alpha x$, so $U''(0) = U_0\alpha^2$. Then $\omega = \sqrt{U_0\alpha^2/m} = \alpha\sqrt{U_0/m}$. Answer: C. (You could also use $1 - \cos\theta \approx \theta^2/2$ for small $\theta$.)

Sudden changes: catch the moment

A favourite exam setup: something lands on, or breaks off, an oscillating block. Use two rules.

  1. During the instant of the change, momentum is conserved (for a block landing or sticking) and the position doesn’t jump.
  2. Afterwards, it is a new SHM with the new mass: $\omega' = \sqrt{k/m'}$ and $A'^2 = x^2 + v'^2/\omega'^2$.

A mass $M$ on a horizontal spring oscillates with amplitude $A_1$. As $M$ passes through its mean position, a smaller mass $m$ is placed gently on it, and the two then move together with amplitude $A_2$. The ratio $A_1/A_2$ is $\sqrt{\dfrac{M+m}{M}}$ $\dfrac{M+m}{M}$ $\dfrac{M}{M+m}$ $\sqrt{\dfrac{M}{M+m}}$ At the mean position, $A = v_{\max}/\omega$. Find the new $v$ from momentum and the new $\omega$ from the new mass.

Before: $A_1 = v/\omega$ with $\omega = \sqrt{k/M}$. Momentum gives $v' = \dfrac{Mv}{M+m}$, and $\omega' = \sqrt{k/(M+m)}$. So

$$A_2 = \frac{v'}{\omega'} = \frac{Mv}{M+m}\sqrt{\frac{M+m}{k}} = \frac{Mv}{\sqrt{k(M+m)}}, \qquad \frac{A_1}{A_2} = \frac{v\sqrt{M/k}}{Mv/\sqrt{k(M+m)}} = \sqrt{\frac{M+m}{M}}.$$

Answer: A.

 A block on a light spring oscillates with amplitude $A$ on a smooth horizontal surface. As it passes through its equilibrium position, half of its mass breaks off and is left behind. The amplitude of the remaining system becomes $fA$. Find $f$ (2 decimal places). The piece that stays on the spring keeps its speed $v = A\omega$, but its mass halves.

Just before the break, $v = A\omega = A\sqrt{k/m}$. The remaining half keeps this speed, and its new $\omega' = \sqrt{2k/m} = \sqrt2\,\omega$. Since it is at the mean position, $A' = v/\omega' = A\omega/(\sqrt2\,\omega) = A/\sqrt2$. So $f = 1/\sqrt2 \approx 0.71$.

Check with energy: the spring’s energy is the KE of what remains, $\tfrac12(m/2)v^2 = \tfrac12\cdot\tfrac12kA^2 = \tfrac12kA'^2$, so $A'^2 = A^2/2$. ✓

$U = \tfrac12kx^2$, $K = \tfrac12k(A^2 - x^2)$, $E = \tfrac12m\omega^2A^2$, constant. They are equal at $x = \pm A/\sqrt2$. Each energy oscillates at $2f$ and averages $E/2$. Near any minimum, $k = U''(x_0)$. For sudden changes, conserve momentum and keep the position fixed, then restart the SHM.
