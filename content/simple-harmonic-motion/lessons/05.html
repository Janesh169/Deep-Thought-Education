The clock that changed the world

Before 1656 the best mechanical clocks drifted by many minutes a day. That year Christiaan Huygens used Galileo’s discovery, that a pendulum keeps a steady beat, to build the first pendulum clock. Its error dropped to seconds a day. For almost three centuries the most accurate clocks on Earth were pendulums.

In this lesson you will derive why a pendulum keeps time, find where that stops being true, and see how lifts, cars, liquids and even the weather change the beat.

Deriving T = 2π√(L/g)

A bob of mass $m$ hangs on a light string of length $L$. Pull it aside by an angle $\theta$. Along the arc, the only force is the tangential part of its weight, and it points back towards the bottom:

$$m\frac{d^2s}{dt^2} = -mg\sin\theta, \qquad s = L\theta \;\Rightarrow\; \frac{d^2\theta}{dt^2} = -\frac{g}{L}\sin\theta.$$

That is not SHM yet, because of the $\sin\theta$. But for small angles, $\sin\theta \approx \theta$ in radians, and then

$$\frac{d^2\theta}{dt^2} = -\frac{g}{L}\,\theta \qquad\Rightarrow\qquad \omega = \sqrt{\frac gL}, \quad T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac Lg}.$$

Moment of inertia $I = mL^2$; the torque of gravity is $\tau = -mgL\sin\theta$. Then $I\ddot\theta = \tau$ gives $\ddot\theta = -(g/L)\sin\theta \approx -(g/L)\theta$. The torque method generalises to any swinging body, as you will see at the end of the lesson.

The mass cancels completely: a heavy bob and a light bob on equal strings keep the same time. A seconds pendulum, with $T = 2\ \text{s}$ (one second per swing), needs $L = g/\pi^2 \approx 0.99\ \text{m}$. That is why old clock pendulums are about a metre long.

Effective gravity

In an accelerating frame, a pendulum doesn’t feel $g$. It feels the effective gravity: real gravity minus the frame’s acceleration, $\vec g_{\text{eff}} = \vec g - \vec a$. Then simply use $T = 2\pi\sqrt{L/g_{\text{eff}}}$.

What counts as the length

$L$ is the distance from the pivot to the centre of mass of the bob, not to the bottom of the bob. Anything that moves the centre of mass changes the period:

  • A child standing up on a swing raises their centre of mass, so $L$ falls and the period gets shorter.
  • A hollow bob full of water, leaking from a hole at the bottom: as the water drains, the centre of mass first drops (the water left is low down), so $T$ increases. Once nearly empty, the centre of mass rises back to the centre of the shell, so $T$ decreases again.
  • Temperature: a metal rod expands when warm. $L$ grows by $L\alpha\Delta\theta$, so $T$ grows by the fraction $\tfrac12\alpha\Delta\theta$ and the clock loses time in summer.

 A pendulum clock with a brass rod ($\alpha = 2\times10^{-5}\ {}^\circ\text{C}^{-1}$) keeps correct time at 20 °C. How many seconds per day does it lose at 40 °C? (1 decimal place.) $\dfrac{\Delta T}{T} = \tfrac12\alpha\Delta\theta$. A day has 86 400 s.

$\dfrac{\Delta T}{T} = \tfrac12 \times 2\times10^{-5} \times 20 = 2\times10^{-4}$. Each tick takes a little longer, so in a day the clock falls behind by $86\,400 \times 2\times10^{-4} \approx 17.3\ \text{s}$.

A wire that stretches

If the string is a wire with Young’s modulus $Y$ and cross-section $A$, the weight stretches it by $\Delta L = \dfrac{MgL}{AY}$. A heavier bob means a longer wire and a slightly longer period. Since $T \propto \sqrt{L}$, timing two different bobs lets you measure $Y$.

 A pendulum made of a uniform wire of cross-sectional area $A$ has period $T$. When an extra mass $M$ is added to its bob, the period becomes $T_M$. If $Y$ is the Young’s modulus of the wire, then $1/Y$ equals $\left[\left(\dfrac{T_M}{T}\right)^2 - 1\right]\dfrac{A}{Mg}$ $\left[\left(\dfrac{T_M}{T}\right)^2 - 1\right]\dfrac{Mg}{A}$ $\left[1 - \left(\dfrac{T_M}{T}\right)^2\right]\dfrac{A}{Mg}$ $\left[1 - \left(\dfrac{T}{T_M}\right)^2\right]\dfrac{A}{Mg}$ $(T_M/T)^2 = (L + \Delta L)/L$ with $\Delta L = MgL/(AY)$.

$\left(\dfrac{T_M}{T}\right)^2 = \dfrac{L + \Delta L}{L} = 1 + \dfrac{Mg}{AY}$. Rearranging: $\dfrac1Y = \left[\left(\dfrac{T_M}{T}\right)^2 - 1\right]\dfrac{A}{Mg}$. Answer: A.

Swinging solid bodies

A real body swinging about a pivot, such as a rod, a ring or a ruler, is a physical (compound) pendulum. Let $d$ be the distance from the pivot to the centre of mass and $I$ the moment of inertia about the pivot. The torque gives $I\ddot\theta = -mgd\,\theta$ for small angles, so

$$T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{I}{mgd}}.$$
