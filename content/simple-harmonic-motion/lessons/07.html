The bridge that wobbled

On 10 June 2000, thousands of people walked across London’s new Millennium Bridge on its opening day. It began to sway from side to side. As it swayed, people adjusted their steps to keep their balance, and those steps fell into time with the sway, pushing it further. The bridge closed two days later and reopened in 2002, after engineers fitted dozens of dampers.

That story contains both ideas in this lesson. Damping drains an oscillation’s energy. Driving feeds it in, and when the pushes arrive at the natural rhythm, the result is resonance.

Damped oscillations

Real oscillators lose energy to air drag and friction. For slow motion, drag is roughly proportional to velocity, $F_d = -bv$, so

$$m\ddot x = -kx - b\dot x.$$

For light damping, the solution is a cosine whose amplitude decays exponentially:

$$x = A_0\,e^{-bt/2m}\cos(\omega' t + \phi), \qquad \omega' = \sqrt{\omega_0^2 - \left(\frac{b}{2m}\right)^2} \approx \omega_0.$$
  • The amplitude goes as $e^{-bt/2m}$. In equal times it shrinks by equal fractions: if it falls to 90% in 5 s, it falls to 81% after 10 s and 72.9% after 15 s.
  • The energy goes as amplitude squared, so $E = E_0e^{-bt/m}$. Energy dies twice as fast as amplitude.
  • Heavier damping gives a slightly slower oscillation. Critical damping returns to rest fastest with no overshoot, which is what car shock absorbers and door closers are designed for. Heavy damping just creeps back.
 The amplitude of a damped oscillator falls to 0.9 times its original value in 5 s. In another 10 s it will fall to $\alpha$ times its original value, where $\alpha$ is 0.7 0.81 0.729 0.6 Exponential decay: every 5 s multiplies the amplitude by 0.9.

After 15 s from the start, there have been three 5-second steps: $\alpha = 0.9^3 = 0.729$. Answer: C.

 A pendulum has significant amplitude only from $t = 0$ until $t = \tau$, where $\tau$ is the time for the amplitude to fall to $1/e$ of its initial value. Call $\tau$ the average life of the pendulum. The spherical bob suffers a retardation (deceleration from viscous drag) proportional to its velocity, with proportionality constant $b$. Assuming the damping is small, the average life in seconds is $1/b$ $2/b$ $0.693/b$ $b$ Here $b$ is a retardation per unit velocity, i.e. $b$ already equals “$b/m$” in our notation.

The equation is $\ddot x = -\omega^2x - b\dot x$, so the amplitude goes as $e^{-bt/2}$. It reaches $1/e$ when $bt/2 = 1$, i.e. $\tau = 2/b$. Answer: B.

The experiment: a pendulum running down

This experiment is in the current JEE Main practical-skills list. Set a pendulum swinging, then read its amplitude (from a scale behind the bob) every few oscillations, and tabulate time against amplitude.

  1. Energy goes as $A^2$, so plot $A^2$ against $t$. The curve falls exponentially: the energy loss per swing is proportional to the energy left.
  2. To check, plot $\ln(A^2)$ against $t$. It is a straight line of slope $-b/m$.
  3. Read off the time for $A^2$ to halve. It is the same whichever point you start from, just like a radioactive half-life.

The decay rate $b/m$ depends on the mass. A heavier bob of the same size, with the same drag, has a smaller $b/m$ and swings for longer. That is why a hollow plastic bob dies down quickly and a solid brass one keeps going.

 In the pendulum-dissipation experiment, $A^2$ falls from 16 cm² to 8 cm² in 60 s. How long, in seconds, from the start does it take $A^2$ to reach 2 cm²?

$A^2$ halves every 60 s, and $16 \to 8 \to 4 \to 2$ is three halvings, so $3 \times 60 = 180\ \text{s}$.

Have you ever pushed someone on a swing? You never push hard. You push at the right moment, just as it starts to move away from you. Hold that picture for three breaths: it is the whole idea of resonance.

Forced oscillations and resonance

Now shake the oscillator with a periodic force, $F_0\sin\omega t$, where $\omega$ is the driving frequency, which is not the same as the natural $\omega_0 = \sqrt{k/m}$. At first the motion is a messy mixture. Once the natural part has been damped away, the body settles into a steady state:

  • it oscillates at the driving frequency $\omega$, not at its own;
  • its amplitude depends on how close $\omega$ is to $\omega_0$:
$$A = \frac{F_0/m}{\sqrt{(\omega_0^2 - \omega^2)^2 + (b\omega/m)^2}}.$$

When $\omega$ is near $\omega_0$, the denominator is small and the amplitude becomes large. That is resonance. The less damping there is, the taller and narrower the peak. With no damping at all, it would grow without limit.

The response lags behind the drive. It is in step at low frequency, a quarter-cycle ($90°$) behind at resonance, and almost opposite ($180°$) at high frequency.

Things to try. (1) Press Start from rest with the drive at 1.00 ω₀ and watch the swing grow, pass after pass, until damping balances the input. (2) Sweep the driving frequency slowly from 0.2 to 2.5. The block stays small, peaks at resonance, then barely moves at high frequency. (3) Lower the damping and the peak gets taller and narrower (higher Q). (4) Press Pluck, driver off to see a free, damped oscillation inside its exponential envelope.

 A particle of mass $m$ on a spring of constant $k$ has natural angular frequency $\omega_0$. An external force $F(t)$ proportional to $\cos\omega t$ ($\omega \ne \omega_0$) acts on it. Ignoring damping, its displacement will be proportional to $\dfrac{1}{m}$ $\dfrac{1}{m(\omega_0^2 - \omega^2)}$ $\dfrac{1}{m(\omega_0^2 + \omega^2)}$ $\dfrac{m}{\omega_0^2 - \omega^2}$

Try $x = C\cos\omega t$ in $m\ddot x + kx = F_0\cos\omega t$: $C(k - m\omega^2) = F_0$, so $C = \dfrac{F_0}{m(\omega_0^2 - \omega^2)}$. Answer: B. It blows up as $\omega \to \omega_0$, which is resonance with no damping.

 For a lightly damped oscillator driven by a sinusoidal force, which statements are true in the steady state? The amplitude is largest when the driving frequency is close to the natural frequency Less damping gives a taller and sharper resonance peak The body oscillates at its own natural frequency, whatever the driving frequency At exact resonance ($\omega = \omega_0$), the displacement lags the driving force by a quarter-cycle

A, B and D are true. C is false: in the steady state the body always follows the driving frequency. Its natural frequency only decides how big the response is.

Resonance in the real world

  • Swings and bridges: small, well-timed pushes add up. Soldiers break step when they cross a bridge so that their marching can’t act as a periodic drive.
  • Radios: tuning means adjusting a circuit’s natural frequency so that it resonates with one station and ignores the rest.
  • Music: a guitar’s hollow body, a violin’s wood and the air in a flute all resonate. That is how a small vibrating string fills a room. You will see this in the next chapter: the resonance tube, organ pipes and strings are all resonance.
  • A wine glass can be shattered by a sustained note at its natural frequency.
