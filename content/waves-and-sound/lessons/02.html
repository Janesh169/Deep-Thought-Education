A snapshot and a movie

There are two ways to look at a wave. Take a photograph and you see the shape of the whole string at one instant: displacement against position. Or film one particle and you see it bob up and down: displacement against time. One equation, $y(x, t)$, holds both pictures. This lesson is about reading it fluently.

Any shape on the move

Take any shape $y = f(x)$ and slide it to the right at speed $v$. After time $t$, the point that was at $x$ is at $x + vt$, so the shape is now $y = f(x - vt)$. In the same way, $f(x + vt)$ slides to the left.

A function of $(x - vt)$ travels towards $+x$. A function of $(x + vt)$ travels towards $-x$. The speed is the coefficient of $t$ divided by the coefficient of $x$.

 The transverse displacement of a wave on a string is $y(x, t) = e^{-(ax^2 + bt^2 + 2\sqrt{ab}\,xt)}$. This represents a wave moving in the $+x$ direction with speed $\sqrt{a/b}$ wave moving in the $-x$ direction with speed $\sqrt{b/a}$ standing wave of frequency $\sqrt b$ standing wave of frequency $1/\sqrt b$ The exponent is a perfect square.

$ax^2 + bt^2 + 2\sqrt{ab}\,xt = (\sqrt a\,x + \sqrt b\,t)^2 = a\left(x + \sqrt{b/a}\,t\right)^2$. So $y$ is a function of $x + \sqrt{b/a}\,t$: a Gaussian pulse moving towards $-x$ at speed $\sqrt{b/a}$. Answer: B.

Reading the direction: if $x$ and $t$ have opposite signs in the phase ($kx - \omega t$ or $\omega t - kx$), the wave moves towards $+x$. If they have the same sign ($kx + \omega t$), it moves towards $-x$. In every case, $v = (\text{coefficient of } t)/(\text{coefficient of } x)$.

Phase difference

Two points a distance $\Delta x$ apart on the same wave do the same motion, but one is a little behind the other. Their phase difference is

$$\Delta\phi = k\,\Delta x = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda}\,\Delta x.$$

So points $\lambda$ apart move exactly in step, and points $\lambda/2$ apart move exactly opposite. In the same way, one particle at two times $\Delta t$ apart differs in phase by $\omega\,\Delta t = 2\pi\Delta t/T$.

 A wave of frequency 500 Hz travels at 360 m/s. What is the phase difference, in degrees, between two points 12 cm apart along its direction of travel?

$\lambda = 360/500 = 0.72\ \text{m}$. $\Delta\phi = 360° \times 0.12/0.72 = 60°$.

Interlace your fingers, push your palms away from you and hold for one breath. Then turn your head slowly left and right. Back to the last idea: what each particle is doing.

Particle velocity is not wave velocity

The wave moves at $v = \omega/k$, a constant set by the medium. Each particle moves up and down in SHM with

$$v_p = \frac{\partial y}{\partial t} = -A\omega\cos(kx - \omega t), \qquad v_{p,\max} = A\omega, \qquad a_p = -\omega^2 y.$$

Comparing $\partial y/\partial t$ with the slope $\partial y/\partial x = Ak\cos(kx - \omega t)$ gives a neat link:

$$v_p = -v\,\frac{\partial y}{\partial x}.$$

For a wave moving to the right, a particle where the string slopes upward (to the right) is moving down, and one on a downward slope is moving up. You can check it with the ghost in the lab: the part of the wave that is about to arrive is the part to the particle’s left. Particles at crests and troughs are momentarily at rest, and particles crossing the middle move fastest.

 A wave travels in the $+x$ direction along a string. At one instant, point P is where the string slopes upward to the right, halfway between a trough and the next crest. At this instant P is moving up moving down at rest moving to the right with the wave

$v_p = -v\,\partial y/\partial x$. The slope is positive and $v > 0$, so $v_p < 0$: P is moving down. Picture the wave sliding right: the trough behind P is about to arrive. Answer: B.

 For the wave $y = 0.02\sin\left[2\pi\left(\dfrac{x}{0.5} - \dfrac{t}{0.01}\right)\right]$ (SI units), find the ratio of the greatest particle speed to the wave speed (2 decimal places). $v_{p,\max}/v = A\omega/(\omega/k) = Ak$.

The wave equation

Every travelling wave $f(x \pm vt)$, whatever its shape, satisfies one partial differential equation:

$$\frac{\partial^2 y}{\partial x^2} = \frac{1}{v^2}\,\frac{\partial^2 y}{\partial t^2}.$$

If you are given an equation for $y(x, t)$ and asked whether it is a wave, check this, or check whether $x$ and $t$ appear only in the combination $x \pm vt$. A function like $y = \sin x\cos t$ also satisfies it; that is a standing wave, which is Lesson 5.

$f(x - vt)$ moves towards $+x$. $y = A\sin(kx - \omega t)$ with $k = 2\pi/\lambda$, $\omega = 2\pi f$, $v = \omega/k$. Opposite signs of $x$ and $t$ mean $+x$; same signs mean $-x$. $\Delta\phi = 2\pi\Delta x/\lambda$. Particle velocity $v_p = -v\,\partial y/\partial x$, with $v_{p,\max} = A\omega$, has nothing to do with the wave speed.
