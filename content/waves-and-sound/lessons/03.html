Counting to the storm

You see the lightning, then you count until the thunder arrives. Sound covers about a kilometre every 3 seconds, so a 6-second gap puts the storm about 2 km away. Light arrives almost instantly, while sound plods along at about 340 m/s.

So what decides how fast a wave goes? For every mechanical wave the answer has the same shape:

$$v = \sqrt{\frac{\text{how hard the medium springs back}}{\text{how much inertia it has}}}.$$

A stiffer medium pulls displaced bits back faster, and a heavier one responds more slowly. This lesson fills in that fraction for strings and for sound.

Waves on a string

For a string under tension $T$ with mass per unit length $\mu$:

$$v = \sqrt{\frac{T}{\mu}}.$$

Run alongside the pulse at speed $v$. In your frame the string flows backwards through a stationary bump. Near the top of the bump, a small piece of length $\Delta l$ follows an arc of radius $R$. Its mass is $\mu\Delta l$ and it moves at $v$, so it needs a centripetal force $\mu\Delta l\,v^2/R$. That force comes from the tension pulling at both ends, slightly downward: together $2T\sin(\Delta l/2R) \approx T\Delta l/R$. Setting them equal: $T\Delta l/R = \mu\Delta l\,v^2/R$, so $v = \sqrt{T/\mu}$. The size and shape of the bump cancel, which is why every pulse on a string travels at the same speed.

Quadruple the tension and the speed doubles. Use a string four times heavier per metre and the speed halves. For a wire of density $\rho$ and cross-section $A$, $\mu = \rho A$. If the tension comes from stretching it, $T = YA \times \text{strain}$.

A rope hanging under its own weight

In a uniform rope hanging freely, the tension at height $y$ above the bottom only has to hold up the rope below it: $T = \mu g y$. So

$$v = \sqrt{\frac{T}{\mu}} = \sqrt{gy},$$

whatever the rope is made of. A pulse sent up from the bottom keeps speeding up. Writing $dy/dt = \sqrt{gy}$ and integrating, the climb to the top of a rope of length $L$ takes $t = 2\sqrt{L/g}$.

Hum softly for one long breath out and feel the buzz in your chest and nose. Those are longitudinal waves in bone and tissue, which carry sound about four times faster than air does.

The speed of sound: Newton’s mistake, Laplace’s fix

For longitudinal waves the “springiness” is an elastic modulus: Young’s modulus $Y$ in a solid rod, and the bulk modulus $B$ in a fluid.

$$v_{\text{solid}} = \sqrt{\frac{Y}{\rho}}, \qquad v_{\text{fluid}} = \sqrt{\frac{B}{\rho}}.$$

Newton (1687) assumed the compressions in a sound wave happen at constant temperature. For an isothermal gas, $B = P$, giving $v = \sqrt{P/\rho} \approx 280\ \text{m/s}$ at 0 °C. Measurements gave about 330 m/s, a 15% error that stood for over a century.

Laplace (1816) saw the problem. The compressions and rarefactions come and go so fast that heat has no time to flow between them, so they are adiabatic. For an adiabatic gas, $B = \gamma P$:

$$v = \sqrt{\frac{\gamma P}{\rho}} = \sqrt{\frac{\gamma RT}{M}} \approx 331\ \text{m/s at } 0\,^\circ\text{C for air } (\gamma = 1.4).$$

 At what temperature, in °C, is the speed of sound in a gas twice its value at 0 °C?

$v \propto \sqrt T$, so doubling $v$ needs $T = 4 \times 273 = 1092\ \text{K}$, which is $819\ ^\circ\text{C}$.

The energy a wave carries

Every particle the wave passes through is an oscillator with energy $\tfrac12(\Delta m)\omega^2A^2$. The wave delivers that energy along at speed $v$. The power crossing each square metre, the intensity, is

$$I = \tfrac12\rho v\omega^2A^2 = 2\pi^2\rho v f^2A^2 \qquad\Rightarrow\qquad I \propto A^2f^2.$$
  • Double the amplitude and you carry four times the energy.
  • From a small source, energy spreads over spheres of area $4\pi r^2$, so $I \propto 1/r^2$ and $A \propto 1/r$.
  • Loudness is measured in decibels, $\beta = 10\log_{10}(I/I_0)$ with $I_0 = 10^{-12}\ \text{W m}^{-2}$. Every 10× in intensity adds 10 dB.
 The intensity of a sound is increased by a factor of 20. By how many decibels does the sound level go up? (1 decimal place is enough.)

$\Delta\beta = 10\log_{10}20 = 10 \times 1.301 \approx 13.0\ \text{dB}$.

String: $v = \sqrt{T/\mu}$. Hanging rope: $v = \sqrt{gy}$, climb time $2\sqrt{L/g}$. Sound: $v = \sqrt{Y/\rho}$ in a solid rod and $\sqrt{B/\rho}$ in a fluid. In a gas $v = \sqrt{\gamma P/\rho} = \sqrt{\gamma RT/M}$ (Laplace); it depends on $T$, but not on $P$ at fixed $T$. Intensity $I \propto A^2f^2$.
