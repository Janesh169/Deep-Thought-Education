Silence from sound

Noise-cancelling headphones use a small microphone to listen to the noise outside. A chip then plays the same sound back through the speaker, exactly upside down: a compression wherever the noise has a rarefaction. The two waves add to almost nothing, and the drone of an aircraft engine fades away.

That works because of one simple rule.

When two or more waves pass through the same place, the total displacement is the sum of the individual displacements: $y = y_1 + y_2 + \dots$ Afterwards, each wave carries on exactly as if the other had never been there.

Pulses passing through each other

Send a pulse from each end of a rope. Where they overlap, the rope’s shape is the sum of the two. Two upright pulses make one twice as tall for a moment. An upright pulse and an inverted one cancel, and the rope is flat for an instant. Then both pulses emerge unchanged and carry on.

When they cancel, where does the energy go? The rope is flat, so there is no stretching energy, but it is moving: all the energy is kinetic for that instant.

Things to try. (1) Watch two upright pulses add, then pass through each other. (2) Flip the second pulse and they cancel for an instant. (3) Try the other modes now or after the reflection section below: Fixed end, Free end and the two joints.

Two waves of the same frequency

When two waves of the same frequency arrive at a point with a phase difference $\phi$, add them like the SHMs in the previous chapter, as rotating arrows:

$$A^2 = A_1^2 + A_2^2 + 2A_1A_2\cos\phi, \qquad I = I_1 + I_2 + 2\sqrt{I_1I_2}\cos\phi.$$
  • Constructive ($\phi = 0, 2\pi, 4\pi \dots$): $A = A_1 + A_2$, the loudest possible.
  • Destructive ($\phi = \pi, 3\pi \dots$): $A = |A_1 - A_2|$, the quietest.
$$\frac{I_{\max}}{I_{\min}} = \left(\frac{A_1 + A_2}{A_1 - A_2}\right)^2 = \left(\frac{\sqrt{I_1} + \sqrt{I_2}}{\sqrt{I_1} - \sqrt{I_2}}\right)^2.$$

For two sources in phase, the phase difference at a point comes from the path difference $\Delta = |r_1 - r_2|$: $\phi = 2\pi\Delta/\lambda$. Loud where $\Delta = n\lambda$, quiet where $\Delta = (n + \tfrac12)\lambda$. If the sources are out of phase, the two rules swap.

Things to try. (1) Turn on the Loudness map. The dark spokes are the quiet lines (nodal lines), where the path difference is a half-odd number of wavelengths. (2) Drag the microphone onto a bright band and onto a dark one, and read the path difference each time. (3) Pull the sources apart, or shorten the wavelength, and more bands appear. (4) Make the sources out of phase: loud and quiet swap places.

 Two coherent sound waves with intensities $I$ and $4I$ overlap. What is the ratio $I_{\max}/I_{\min}$?

The amplitudes are in the ratio $1 : 2$. So $\dfrac{I_{\max}}{I_{\min}} = \left(\dfrac{1 + 2}{2 - 1}\right)^2 = 9$.

 Two loudspeakers driven in phase emit a 680 Hz tone ($v = 340$ m/s). A listener is 3.00 m from one speaker and 4.25 m from the other. The sound there is loud: constructive interference quiet: destructive interference in between impossible to tell without the amplitudes

$\lambda = 340/680 = 0.5\ \text{m}$. The path difference is $1.25\ \text{m} = 2.5\lambda$, a half-odd number of wavelengths, so the interference is destructive. Answer: B. If the speakers are equally loud there, it is nearly silent.

 Two waves $y_1 = a\sin(\omega t + \pi/6)$ and $y_2 = a\cos\omega t$ are superposed. The amplitude of the resultant is $a$ $2a$ $\sqrt3\,a$ $\sqrt2\,a$

$\cos\omega t = \sin(\omega t + \pi/2)$, so the phase difference is $\pi/2 - \pi/6 = \pi/3$. $A^2 = a^2 + a^2 + 2a^2\cos 60° = 3a^2$, so $A = \sqrt3\,a$. Answer: C.

Reflection

A wave arriving at a boundary is partly or wholly reflected. What the reflection looks like depends on what the boundary lets the medium do.

BoundaryReflected waveTransmitted wave
Fixed (rigid) end: a string clamped to a wallinverted, phase change $\pi$none
Free end: a ring sliding on a smooth rodupright, no phase changenone
Light string → heavier string (slower medium)partly, invertedupright, slower, shorter λ
Heavy string → lighter string (faster medium)partly, uprightupright, faster, longer λ

Why does a fixed end invert the pulse? The end can’t move, so the reflected wave must cancel the incoming one there at every instant, and only an inverted copy does that. The frequency never changes on reflection or transmission. The speed and wavelength change in the new medium.

For sound in a pipe, a closed end acts like a fixed end for the air’s displacement. An open end acts roughly like a free end. That gives the organ pipes of Lesson 6.

Compare Fixed end with Free end: one comes back upside down, the other the right way up. At a joint, part of the pulse goes on. Into a heavier string, the reflection is inverted and the transmitted pulse is slower and narrower. Into a lighter string, everything stays upright and the transmitted pulse is faster and wider.

 A wave $y = a\sin(\omega t - kx)$ on a string meets another wave, and together they produce a node at $x = 0$. The equation of the other wave is $y = a\sin(\omega t + kx)$ $y = -a\sin(\omega t + kx)$ $y = a\sin(\omega t - kx)$ $y = -a\sin(\omega t - kx)$ A node at $x = 0$ means the total displacement there is zero at every instant.

The second wave must travel the other way (so $\omega t + kx$) and must exactly cancel the first at $x = 0$: $a\sin\omega t + y_2(0, t) = 0$ for all $t$. That gives $y_2 = -a\sin(\omega t + kx)$. This is exactly a wave reflected from a fixed end at $x = 0$. Answer: B.

 A pulse travels along a light string into a much heavier string joined to it (same tension). Which statements are true? The reflected pulse is inverted The transmitted pulse is inverted The transmitted pulse moves more slowly than the incident one Any wave crossing the joint keeps its frequency

A, C, D. The heavy string behaves almost like a wall, so the reflection is inverted. The transmitted pulse is always upright. $v = \sqrt{T/\mu}$ is smaller in the heavy string, and the frequency is set by the source, so it is unchanged.

Superposition: displacements add, and the waves pass through each other unchanged. For the same frequency, $A^2 = A_1^2 + A_2^2 + 2A_1A_2\cos\phi$ and $\phi = 2\pi\Delta/\lambda$. Loud at $\Delta = n\lambda$, quiet at $(n + \tfrac12)\lambda$. $I_{\max}/I_{\min} = ((A_1 + A_2)/(A_1 - A_2))^2$. Fixed end or slower medium: reflection inverted. Free end or faster medium: upright. Frequency never changes.
